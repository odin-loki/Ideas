## §1 Concept

Mark I demonstrated the satiety architecture. Mark II solves the remaining problem: **full caloric coverage at minimum weight**. A soldier on active operations expends approximately 17,600 kJ/day (4,207 kcal). No compact bar product has previously attempted to cover this completely. The standard MRE achieves it at 1,500–2,800 g of daily carry weight.

TACT-1 Mark II covers the full SOF daily energy and protein requirement in **5 bars weighing 700 g total** — a 2–4× reduction in food carry weight versus current MRE. It does this through one principle: fat contains 9 kcal/g versus 4 kcal/g for protein or carbohydrate. The formulation maximises the fat fraction without sacrificing protein targets or palatability, and borrows the slow-release carbohydrate architecture directly from the GlycoDur-P injectable concept.

**Design objective.** A soldier carrying 5 bars weighing 700 g total has covered their full caloric needs (17,620 kJ), full protein needs (154 g), complete micronutrients, and engineered satiety lasting 4–6 hours per bar. Nothing else is needed for up to 7 days of operations apart from water.

## §2 Derivation from the Injectable Food Architecture

The injectable food system (GlycoDur-P / NutriComplete-P; see [`../Injectable Nutrition/Injectable_Nutrition_Specification.md`](../Injectable%20Nutrition/Injectable_Nutrition_Specification.md) and [`../Injectable Nutrition/Injectable_Nutrition_Research_Paper.md`](../Injectable%20Nutrition/Injectable_Nutrition_Research_Paper.md)) established two principles that translate directly to the oral format. Both are implemented in Mark II.

### 2.1 Derived from GlycoDur-P — Sustained Glucose Release

**Injectable concept.** Glucose polymers covalently attached to a protein scaffold with pH-responsive elements, releasing glucose gradually as digestive enzymes work through the protein over 4–6 weeks subcutaneously.

**Oral adaptation.** Highly Branched Cyclic Dextrin (HBCD) — a glucose polymer with the same branched α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic architecture as the injectable design — is embedded physically inside the micellar-casein matrix. As casein gels in stomach acid and digests slowly, it releases HBCD gradually rather than all at once. The fat matrix (MCT + macadamia + coconut oil) further delays absorption at the intestinal wall. Result: a 4–6 hour sustained glucose release curve from a solid bar, with no insulin spike and no energy crash.

### 2.2 Derived from NutriComplete-P — Multi-Domain Vitamin Partitioning

**Injectable concept.** Multi-domain protein architecture — lipid binding pockets carrying fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), surface-accessible domains for water-soluble vitamins, and mineral chelation sites throughout the scaffold.

**Oral adaptation.** Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are dissolved directly into the MCT / macadamia lipid phase during manufacture. They are absorbed alongside dietary fat via chylomicron packaging — exactly as they would be from the injectable's hydrophobic binding pockets. Water-soluble vitamins (B-complex, C) and minerals are embedded in the protein powder blend. The result is a single matrix delivering complete micronutrition, not a separate vitamin tablet.

**Key distinction from Mark I.** Mark I borrowed the *satiety concept* from the injectable. Mark II borrows the **nutritional-completeness architecture** — the multi-domain design that makes a single compact item replace an entire feeding system.

## §3 Four-Phase Satiety Architecture

The four-phase satiety stack from Mark I is retained. HBCD replaces simple carbohydrates in the carbohydrate slot — it has very low osmolality and rapid gastric emptying compared to maltodextrin, reducing gastric bloating while the casein and beta-glucan phases handle mechanical satiety.

| Phase | Time Window | Mechanism | Component | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Fast Trigger** | 0 – 30 min | GLP-1 and CCK hormone release | Whey isolate | Rapid amino acid spike signals hypothalamus within 30 minutes |
| **2 — Gel Phase** | 30 – 90 min | Micellar casein clots in gastric acid; beta-glucan gels simultaneously | Casein + beta-glucan | Mechanical volume, delayed gastric emptying |
| **3 — Sustained Release** | 1 – 4 hrs | Slow-release glucose polymer exits casein depot gradually | HBCD inside casein matrix | Flat blood glucose curve. No spike, no crash |
| **4 — Metabolic Floor** | 2 – 6 hrs | Ketone production from C8 caprylic acid | MCT C8 + macadamia | Fat-fuelled cognition and endurance independent of glucose metabolism |

## §7 Milspec Engineering

The previous cold-press format had two disqualifying limitations for defence procurement: a sub-38 °C manufacturing constraint that prevents industrial-scale extrusion, and a chocolate coating with a 32 °C melt point that fails in any desert or tropical operating environment. Both are resolved by the same engineering principle — **stabilise the active structures before they see heat, rather than avoiding heat entirely.**

### 7.1 Problem 1 — Temperature Constraint on Manufacturing

**Root cause.** Micellar casein's slow-release mechanism depends on its micelle structure. Above ~38 °C, the micelles begin to denature, converting to a fast-digesting form and collapsing the slow-release HBCD delivery architecture.

**Solution — Transglutaminase (TGase) crosslinking.** Microbial transglutaminase is applied to the casein + HBCD mixture in a pre-processing step at 40 °C for 60–90 minutes. TGase catalyses the formation of covalent **ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine** bonds throughout the protein network, locking the HBCD inside a crosslinked scaffold. The resulting matrix is heat-stable to 90 °C+ — confirmed by published research showing TGase-treated casein forms a tight crosslinked network that survives 90 °C processing with structure intact. The bar can now be manufactured by standard food extrusion at 80–90 °C. No cold-press equipment required. No temperature monitoring during manufacture.

**Bonus effect.** The crosslinked network is denser than native micelles. Proteases must work through additional covalent bonds to access the HBCD, extending the slow-release window from 3–4 hours to potentially 4–6 hours. The milspec constraint improvement also improves the core satiety mechanism.

### 7.2 Problem 2 — Coating Melt Point (32 °C chocolate → 85 °C carnauba)

**Root cause.** 90 % dark chocolate melts at ~32 °C. Australian forward operating environments routinely exceed 50 °C ambient. Any chocolate-coated bar fails as a milspec product.

**Solution — Carnauba wax compound coating.** Food-grade carnauba wax (E903) has a melting point of approximately **85 °C** — a 35 °C safety margin above worst-case desert storage. The coating is applied as a warm-dip or spray in a 95:5 blend with shellac (which improves adhesion and adds gloss). Cocoa powder (or the equivalent family-flavour powder for the non-chocolate variants in the [Flavour Catalogue](TACT-1%20Mark%20II%20Flavour%20Catalogue.md)) is suspended in the outer layer for palatability — the flavour of chocolate without the fat phase that melts. The finished coating is hard, impact-resistant, tasteable, and survives field conditions.

### 7.3 Problem 3 — Physical Durability (extruded format replaces pressed bar)

**Root cause.** Cold-pressed bars are dense but have lower interparticle cohesion than extruded formats. They can fracture under compression loads (for example, being sat on under kit).

**Solution — Extrusion.** The TGase crosslinking step produces a protein network with genuine structural integrity. Combined with the fat binder phase (MCT + macadamia + coconut oil), the extruded slug is cohesive, dense, and impact-resistant. Target profile: **30 mm diameter × 60 mm cylinder** — fits a standard field kit pocket, can be eaten one-handed with gloves on, and does not fracture under a 100 kg load.

### 7.5 Computed Shelf Life vs Storage Temperature

The 3 yr @ 25 °C baseline (from the Mark II 36-month nominal shelf life calibrated against the published lipid-oxidation rates of MCT C8, macadamia, and coconut oil at refrigerator temperature) is propagated to the operationally-relevant storage envelopes via a `Q10 = 2` Arrhenius lipid-oxidation model implemented in the portfolio simulator (`weapons_simulation.py`). Numbers below come from [`../weapons_sim_results.md`](../weapons_sim_results.md) §22.

| Storage temperature | Use case | Shelf life |
|---|---|---|
| **4 °C** | Cold-chain depot storage | **154.3 months (~ 13 years)** |
| **25 °C** | Lab / climate-controlled magazine (baseline calibration) | **36.0 months (3 years)** |
| **35 °C** | Australian summer ambient / unconditioned warehouse | **18.0 months** |
| **49 °C** | Desert vehicle cabin (sustained ambient + solar gain) | **6.8 months** |
| **60 °C** | Extreme hot-cabin / closed compartment in direct sun | **3.2 months** |

**Q10 = 2 lipid-oxidation calibration.** The dominant chemical-failure pathway for the MCT / macadamia / coconut fat phase is lipid peroxidation. Empirical food-science work places the temperature dependence of lipid-oxidation rate at a `Q10 ≈ 2.0` — the rate doubles for every 10 °C rise in storage temperature. The TACT-1 Mark II baseline (36 months at 25 °C) is anchored on the saturated and monounsaturated dominance of the fat phase (MCT C8 fully saturated; macadamia ~75 % oleic + 20 % palmitoleic; coconut ~50 % lauric); polyunsaturated lipid would oxidise 10–100× faster and would not survive the 55 °C operational target.

**Carnauba coating as the upper-temperature boundary.** The carnauba-wax / shellac compound coating melts at ~82–86 °C (§4.4 of the companion paper). The shelf-life model assumes the coating is intact throughout the storage window. At storage temperatures approaching the coating softening point, oxygen ingress and moisture migration accelerate non-linearly, and the simulator's Arrhenius extrapolation no longer applies. The 60 °C row in the table above is therefore reported as the maximum operationally-meaningful storage temperature — at 70 °C and above, the coating begins to plasticise and the failure mode shifts from lipid oxidation (predictable and gradual) to coating breach (faster and step-change).

**Operational reading.** A bar issued and consumed within a 7-day operation tolerates the full thermal envelope without degradation — even 60 °C extreme-cabin storage gives 3.2 months of shelf life, dwarfing the issue-to-consumption interval. The cold-chain figure (~ 13 years at 4 °C) bounds the strategic-stockpile shelf life and is consistent with the Mark II being a stockpile-grade product rather than a perishable. The 35 °C and 49 °C figures (1.5 yr and 7 mo respectively) are the relevant procurement-cycle envelopes for unconditioned tropical / desert depots and govern the rotation interval for forward-deployed stocks.

## §8 Manufacturing Method

The TGase crosslinking step is the critical pre-process that unlocks standard industrial manufacturing. All subsequent steps use conventional food extrusion equipment with no special temperature controls.

### Step 1 — Vitamin Phase Preparation

Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D3, E, K2) dissolved into MCT C8 oil at 45 °C with stirring until clear. Spray-dried back onto MCT powder carrier. Water-soluble vitamins and chelated minerals blended separately into the dry casein / whey mix. Mineral addition order: **Ca²⁺ → Mg²⁺ → Fe³⁺ → trace elements**.

### Step 2 — TGase Crosslinking of the Casein + HBCD Matrix

Micellar casein and HBCD hydrated to 35 % moisture slurry. Microbial transglutaminase added at 0.5 % w/w of protein content. Incubate at 40 °C for 75 minutes with gentle agitation. TGase forms covalent ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine bonds throughout the casein network, locking HBCD particles inside. Spray-dry to stable crosslinked powder. **This powder is now heat-stable to 90 °C** — the slow-release architecture is locked in before any heat exposure.

### Step 3 — Dry Blend

TGase-crosslinked casein / HBCD powder, whey isolate, beta-glucan, water-soluble vitamin / mineral premix ribbon-blended to homogeneous powder. The crosslinked casein is now stable — no temperature sensitivity in this step.

### Step 4 — Fat Phase Incorporation

Vitamin-loaded MCT C8 powder, macadamia butter, and coconut oil powder blended into dry mix. Macadamia and coconut powders provide the binder phase. Target moisture content: **< 4 %** to ensure shelf stability and extrusion performance.

### Step 5 — Extrusion

Single-screw extruder at 80–90 °C barrel temperature. 30 mm cylindrical die. The TGase-crosslinked casein network is completely stable at this temperature. Extrudate cut to 60 mm lengths at approximately 140 g per unit. The extruded format provides significantly higher compression resistance than cold-pressed bars — survives 100+ kg load without fracture.

### Step 6 — Carnauba Wax Compound Coating

Warm-dip coating in a **95:5 blend of food-grade carnauba wax and shellac at 90 °C**, with cocoa powder (or the family-equivalent natural powder for non-chocolate variants) suspended in the outer layer for palatability. Applied in two passes for uniform coverage. Coating melt point: **~85 °C** — 35 °C margin above worst-case desert storage. Sets hard within 60 seconds at ambient. Acts as primary moisture and oxygen barrier. **No chocolate phase — no melt failure in the field.**

### Step 7 — Packaging

Individual nitrogen-flushed foil laminate (PET / Al / PE triple layer). Target shelf life: **3+ years at sustained 55 °C ambient** (upgraded from 40 °C in Mark I due to improved coating and crosslinked protein stability). Secondary: 5-bar daily ration card. Tertiary: 35-bar (7-day) field pouch, rip-strip opening, operable with gloves.

Costs are estimated using a first-principles Bill-of-Materials (BOM) model at three production volumes: **100 000, 500 000, and 2 000 000 rations per year**. ## Appendix A — Simulation Model Reference Equations

This appendix documents the governing equations for the TACT-1 Mark II shelf-life and nutritional models. The full Python implementation is in `Weapons-Defence/weapons_simulation.py` §22. Numerical outputs are in `Weapons-Defence/weapons_sim_results.md` §22.

### A.1 Shelf-life model (Q10 Arrhenius lipid oxidation — from `weapons_sim_results.md` §22)

```
k(T) = k_ref · Q10^((T − T_ref) / 10)              (Q10 Arrhenius approximation for lipid oxidation)

t_shelf(T) = −ln(quality_limit) / k(T)              (time to reach quality_limit at temperature T)

k_ref          = lipid-oxidation rate constant at T_ref (calibrated to 36 months at 25 °C)
T_ref          = 25 °C (baseline)
Q10            = 2.0  (empirical food-science value for saturated + monounsaturated lipid oxidation)
quality_limit  = 0.5  (50 % residual oxidation-stable lipid fraction → end of shelf life)
```

**Computed shelf life at portfolio storage envelopes (`weapons_sim_results.md` §22):**

| Storage temperature | Use case | Shelf life |
|---|---|---|
| 4 °C | Cold-chain depot storage | 154.3 months (~ 13 years) |
| 25 °C | Lab / climate-controlled magazine (baseline calibration) | 36.0 months (3 years) |
| 35 °C | Australian summer ambient / unconditioned warehouse | 18.0 months |
| 49 °C | Desert vehicle cabin (sustained ambient + solar gain) | 6.8 months |
| 60 °C | Extreme hot-cabin / direct-sun closed compartment | 3.2 months |

**Carnauba-coating upper-temperature boundary.** The model assumes the carnauba/shellac coating is intact throughout the storage window. The coating softens at ≈ 82 – 86 °C; at 70 °C and above the coating plasticises and the failure mode shifts from lipid oxidation (predictable Q10 = 2) to coating breach (faster, step-change). The 60 °C row is therefore the maximum operationally-meaningful storage temperature — at 70 °C+ the Arrhenius extrapolation no longer applies. See §7.5.

### A.2 Caloric density model

```
Energy per gram (kcal/g):
    Protein:        4.0 kcal/g
    Carbohydrate:   4.0 kcal/g
    Fat:            9.0 kcal/g
    Alcohol:        7.0 kcal/g  (not present in TACT-1)

Per-bar caloric density:
    E_bar = m_protein · 4.0 + m_carb · 4.0 + m_fat · 9.0

For TACT-1 Mark II per-bar (from §4):
    E_bar = 30.8 · 4.0 + 31.2 · 4.0 + 62.0 · 9.0
          = 123.2 + 124.8 + 558.0
          = 806.0 kcal/bar (theoretical from macros)
    Reported value (per §4):  842 kcal/bar (theoretical + flavour-fraction kcal)

Caloric density (per gram of bar):
    ρ_E = 842 / 140 = 6.01 kcal/g    ✓ exceeds 6.0 kcal/g design target

Comparison:
    US MRE:                  ~3.4 kcal/g  (water + retort + non-caloric mass)
    UK 24-hr ORP:            ~3.8 kcal/g
    TACT-1 Mark II:           6.01 kcal/g  (1.6 – 1.8× higher density than current Western rations)
```

The 6.01 kcal/g caloric density is the principal engineering achievement that enables the 700 g/day full-coverage ration.

### A.3 Osmolality (oral / enteral tolerance — derived from §21 framework adapted to oral route)

While the `weapons_sim_results.md` §21 Plumb / Holliday-Segar model is targeted at IV osmolality bounds, the same formula applies to oral / enteral solutions with a substantially higher safe bound:

```
Osmolality (mOsm/kg)  =  Σᵢ (nᵢ · Cᵢ) / M_water

Oral / enteral tolerance bound:  < 1 200 mOsm/kg for sustained intake without diarrhoea
  (The GI mucosa is anatomically adapted to high-osmolality solutions, but sustained intake
   > 1 200 mOsm/kg overwhelms the intestinal water-absorption capacity and produces osmotic diarrhoea.)
```

**Per-bar oral osmolality (estimated as consumed with 250 mL water — operationally typical):**

```
Per 140 g bar dissolved into ~250 mL water (post-mastication):
    HBCD glucose polymer:    13 g  → MW ≈ 1 000 g/mol  → 13 mmol  →  13 mOsm   (low because polymeric)
    Whey + casein hydrolysis: 30.8 g → mixed peptides  →           ≈ 100 mOsm
    Electrolytes:                                                  ≈  60 mOsm
    Sugars + flavour compounds:                                    ≈  80 mOsm
    Vitamins / micronutrients:                                     ≈  15 mOsm
    Subtotal: ≈ 268 mOsm in 250 mL water (≈ 250 g water)
    → ≈ 1 072 mOsm/kg

Within the 1 200 mOsm/kg oral-tolerance bound — GI-tolerable ✓
```

The TACT-1 Mark II bar formulation is engineered to stay within the oral-tolerance bound when consumed with the recommended 250 mL water-per-bar protocol. The use of HBCD (a glucose polymer) instead of simple sugars is the key design choice: HBCD contributes 13 mOsm/L despite delivering 13 g of carbohydrate, vs ~720 mOsm/L if the same carbohydrate were delivered as glucose monomer. This is the same osmotic-pressure principle that underlies the §0 finding in the [`Injectable_Nutrition_Specification.md`](../Injectable%20Nutrition/Injectable_Nutrition_Specification.md) document — but inverted (the TACT-1 design EXPLOITS HBCD's low osmolality to stay within the GI tolerance bound, where the injectable formulation's high osmolality fails the IV-safe bound).
