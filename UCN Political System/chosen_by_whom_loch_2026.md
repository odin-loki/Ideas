# Chosen by Whom? Divine Election as Epistemic Privilege and Historical Pathogen

**Author:** Odin Loch  
**Date:** June 2026  
**Classification:** Independent Research — Open Submission

---

## Abstract

The doctrine of divine chosenness — the belief that a particular group, lineage, or individual has been selected by a supreme deity for special purpose, protection, or dominion — is among the oldest and most consequential ideas in recorded human history. This paper argues that it also constitutes one of the most reliably destructive. Across every major civilisational theatre — from the Canaanite conquest to the American frontier, from South African apartheid to contemporary ethnonationalism — the doctrine of divine election has functioned not as a spiritual comfort but as a violence-legitimising mechanism of the first order. It renders the other sub-human, the land transferable, and the killing holy.

The paper further identifies a profound and largely unexamined legal inconsistency at the heart of modern Western jurisprudence: the claim "I am chosen by God" is simultaneously a protected religious expression and a diagnostic symptom of psychiatric disorder, depending entirely upon whether the claimant belongs to an institutionally recognised faith tradition. This asymmetry is not a minor procedural quirk. It is a structural embedding of epistemic privilege that shields the doctrine's most dangerous properties from critical scrutiny while subjecting individuals without institutional backing to coercive medical intervention for asserting functionally identical beliefs.

This paper does not attack any religion or its adherents. It attacks a *class of doctrine* — specifically, the belief structure asserting special divine mandate that excludes, diminishes, or justifies harm to those outside the chosen group — and argues for its recognition as a persistent civilisational pathogen requiring honest confrontation in both public discourse and legal frameworks.

## 1. The Opening Paradox: Same Claim, Different Outcome

Consider the following three statements:

> *"I am chosen by God for a special purpose."*

> *"Our people are God's chosen, with a covenant granting us this land."*

> *"We are the divinely guided community, mandated to bring God's order to the world."*

In contemporary Western society, the first statement — spoken by an individual without institutional religious affiliation, particularly one with a psychiatric history — routinely results in involuntary assessment, forced medication, and detention under mental health legislation. The second is the foundational doctrinal claim of Judaism, legally protected under freedom of religion across all democratic jurisdictions. The third is a mainstream position within strands of both Islam and Christianity, similarly protected.

The logical content of these three statements is structurally identical. Each asserts a special relationship with a divine being. Each claims that this relationship confers purpose, identity, or entitlement. Each is unfalsifiable by empirical means. Yet their social and legal treatment is radically divergent.

This divergence is not incidental. It is codified. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), the governing diagnostic framework for psychiatry across much of the English-speaking world, defines a delusion as "a false belief based on incorrect inference about external reality that is firmly held despite what almost everyone else believes and despite what constitutes incontrovertible and obvious proof to the contrary." Critically, it adds a cultural exemption: the belief must not be "one ordinarily accepted by other members of the person's culture or subculture."

This exemption is not neutral. It does not evaluate the epistemic content of the belief. It evaluates only whether the belief has sufficient institutional backing. A man who claims God has chosen him personally for a divine mission, and who lacks a supporting congregation or tradition, may be sectioned under the Mental Health Act. A man who claims that his ethnic or religious community is collectively chosen by God, backed by a millennia-old institution and billions of adherents, cannot be. The claim is the same. The protection is entirely dependent on market share.

The philosopher Peter Boghossian noted this explicitly: "Currently, the DSM grants religious delusions an exemption from classification as a mental illness." Academic philosopher José Eduardo Porcher, writing in *Filosofia Unisinos* (2018), confirmed the mechanism: the cultural exemption exists because institutional religious beliefs are socially transmitted through recognised cultural processes, not because they are epistemically superior to individual claims. The protection is sociological, not logical.

This paper does not argue that all religious belief should be pathologised. It argues the opposite: that the asymmetric application of epistemic standards — coercive toward the unaffiliated, permissive toward the institutionalised — conceals the genuine harms of divine chosenness doctrines behind a wall of legally enforced cultural deference.

## 2. Defining the Doctrine: What Divine Chosenness Actually Asserts

Divine chosenness, as a doctrinal class, shares a coherent structure across its many cultural manifestations. It asserts:

1. **Selection**: A specific group (or individual) has been singled out by a supreme being.
2. **Distinction**: This selection marks the chosen as categorically different from the unchosen.
3. **Mandate**: The selection carries purpose — typically territorial, civilisational, spiritual, or political.
4. **Legitimacy**: Actions taken in fulfilment of the mandate are sanctioned by the divine selector, placing them beyond ordinary moral accountability.

Biblical scholar Michael Coogan, in his work *God's Favorites: Judaism, Christianity, and the Myth of Divine Chosenness* (published through MIT Press), argues that it is not God who chooses groups but that groups "describe themselves as divinely chosen to rationalise their view of themselves as superior to others." Coogan traces the doctrine from Genesis through its inheritance by Christianity and its subsequent deployment as colonial ideology.

Anthropologists consistently classify divine chosenness as a structural form of ethnocentrism — the attribution of superior status to one's own group, elevated here by cosmic endorsement. What distinguishes religious ethnocentrism from ordinary cultural pride is precisely the divine legitimation: ordinary pride can be contested on equal terms; divinely mandated superiority, by definition, cannot be challenged from within its own framework. The chosen are chosen *by God*. Argument with the chosen is argument with God.

This is the doctrine's most dangerous property: **its structural resistance to correction**.

Karl Jaspers, the German psychiatrist and philosopher, identified three criteria for delusion: certainty (the belief is absolute), incorrigibility (it cannot be revised by evidence), and impossibility or falsity of content. Divine chosenness satisfies all three — yet it is precisely this incorrigibility that has made it historically durable, and legally privileged.

## 3. The Historical Record: Divine Chosenness as Violence Engine

The historical record of divine chosenness is not ambiguous. Across every major instance of its application to territorial or political disputes, the outcome includes systematic violence against those defined as unchosen. The following survey is representative rather than exhaustive.

### 3.1 The Biblical Foundation and the Canaanite Precedent

The oldest sustained example of divine chosenness in the Western tradition is the Israelite covenant described in the Hebrew Bible. The selection of Abraham and his descendants, formalised in the Genesis covenant and operationalised in Exodus through Deuteronomy, establishes the structural template: a specific people, a specific land, and a divine mandate to possess it regardless of existing inhabitants.

The Canaanite conquest is not peripheral to this tradition; it is central to it. The destruction of Canaanite populations — commanded, in the text, by the deity directly — was, as one commentator has argued, the "bedrock of chosenness": proof that Israel's covenant was not abstract but territorial and bloody. The land itself became theological, and possession of it inseparable from divine sanction.

This is not merely ancient history. It established a reusable template: chosen people, promised land, unchosen inhabitants, divine licence to act. This template has been invoked, with minimal structural modification, across three thousand years of subsequent history.

### 3.2 Christian Crusades and the Reconfiguration of Chosenness

Christianity reframed Jewish chosenness in a specifically supersessionist direction: the church, not the Jewish people, had become God's chosen vehicle. The promised land was reconfigured as the Kingdom of Heaven, but the territorial logic was easily re-earthed when politically convenient. Medieval Christendom deployed the Canaanite model directly to justify the Crusades: God's people eradicating God's enemies in God's land. The infidel occupied the structural position of the Canaanite — categorically outside the covenant, and therefore outside full moral consideration.

The Crusades resulted in hundreds of thousands of deaths across two centuries of organised violence. They were not aberrations of the faith. They were logical extensions of the chosenness structure: if we are God's chosen, those who resist our mission resist God.

### 3.3 The Doctrine of Discovery and Colonial Genocide

The most consequential deployment of divine chosenness in the modern era was European colonialism. The Doctrine of Discovery, formalised in a series of papal bulls beginning in the 15th century, declared that lands not inhabited by Christians could be claimed by Christian monarchs acting as God's agents. It provided, as one historian has described it, "the ideological underpinning for European powers to expand their dominion over foreign lands, brutally subjugate indigenous populations, and perpetrate heinous acts of violence."

The Puritan settlers of North America are an instructive case. They believed explicitly that they were divinely chosen to form a biblical commonwealth in the New World. They tolerated no competing faiths. They committed sustained violence against indigenous populations whom they framed, in the Canaanite tradition, as obstacles to divine purpose. This cultural framework — God's chosen people, godless heathens, sanctified dispossession — structured American expansion for the following three centuries.

Manifest Destiny, coined by journalist John L. O'Sullivan in 1845, was the 19th-century American articulation of this framework. It asserted a divinely ordained right and duty for the United States to expand across the continent. Scholars have described it as the ideological foundation for what amounted to genocide: an estimated 50 million indigenous lives lost across the full period of American expansion. The Indian Removal Act of 1830, the Trail of Tears, the Sand Creek Massacre of 1864, and the Wounded Knee Massacre of 1890 were all downstream of this doctrine.

The direct line from divine chosenness to mass killing is not metaphorical. It is historical and documentable. At Sand Creek, militia commander General John Chivington — a Methodist lay preacher — ordered the massacre of a Cheyenne village, killing approximately 70 to 163 people, most of them women and children. He defended his actions explicitly in the framework of divine right. The Doctrine of Discovery was formally disavowed by Pope Francis only in March 2023 — nearly 500 years after its initial promulgation.

### 3.4 South African Apartheid

The theological foundation of South African apartheid is a direct and well-documented product of divine chosenness doctrine. The Dutch Reformed Church, from at least 1618, promoted the Calvinist concept of a chosen people as the basis for racial and communal superiority. The Afrikaner settlers interpreted themselves as a divinely elected nation, the Boers as God's covenant people in Africa, with a theological mandate to maintain racial separation.

Apartheid-era textbooks explicitly reinforced this narrative. The system of racial codification, forced displacement, economic exclusion, and systematic violence that defined South African apartheid between 1948 and 1994 was not primarily the product of secular racism. It was theologically grounded. Its architects believed — sincerely — that racial separation was divinely ordained.

### 3.5 Islamic Divine Mandate

Islam contains its own structural analogue to divine chosenness in the concept of the *ummah* — the global community of believers — as the divinely guided community with a mandate to establish God's order. The Quran (35:32) describes Muslims as those "whom We have chosen," and (21:143) as "a moderate nation" tasked with witnessing against mankind.

The political structure of the Caliphate was premised on the divinely mandated leadership of this community. Historically, the expansion of the early Islamic Caliphates was framed not as conquest but as the fulfilment of divine purpose — bringing the world under God's governance. This framework does not mandate the killing of non-believers, and mainstream Islamic jurisprudence has complex protections for non-Muslim subjects (*dhimmi*). However, the structural logic of divine mandate — that Muslim governance is cosmically superior and that resistance to it is resistance to God — has historically generated violence in conditions of power and conflict, as it does in every tradition where divine chosenness meets political opportunity.

The Islamic State's 2014 proclamation of a renewed Caliphate was an extreme contemporary manifestation of the same structural logic: divine selection, territorial mandate, the unchosen as categorically inferior.

### 3.6 Hindu Nationalism (Hindutva)

The Hindutva movement in India, whose ideological foundations were laid by V.D. Savarkar in the early 20th century, represents a non-Abrahamic variant of divine chosenness ideology. Hindutva posits an essentially Hindu India — *Hindu Rashtra* — in which the Hindu majority is the legitimate, culturally superior heir to the subcontinent, with minority groups (particularly Muslims and Christians) occupying the structural position of the unchosen.

The violence associated with Hindutva's political ascendance — including communal riots, mosque demolitions, anti-conversion legislation, and the phenomenon of cow-vigilante lynching — follows the now-familiar pattern: divine or cultural election providing moral licence for violence against the designated other.

### 3.7 Summary: The Pattern

Across every case examined, the following pattern holds:

- A group asserts divine or cosmic election.
- This election is used to define an outgroup as categorically inferior or threatening.
- The ingroup's political, territorial, or violent actions against the outgroup are framed as fulfilment of the divine mandate.
- The doctrine's incorrigibility — its resistance to falsification — renders ordinary moral argument ineffective against it.
- The outcome is systematic violence, often of genocidal scale.

The doctrine is not merely correlated with violence. It is mechanistically upstream of it. It provides the moral architecture that makes mass killing feel not just permissible but holy.

## 4. The Legal Contradiction: Institutionally Laundered Delusion

### 4.1 How Law Currently Treats Divine Claims

Under the legal frameworks of most liberal democracies, freedom of religion is a fundamental right. Article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) states that "everyone shall have the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion," and that "no one shall be subject to coercion which would impair his freedom to have or to adopt a religion or belief of his choice." This protection is broad. The UN Human Rights Committee has explicitly stated that it covers "theistic, non-theistic and atheistic beliefs."

Simultaneously, mental health legislation in these same jurisdictions permits the involuntary detention and forced medication of individuals whose beliefs are judged by clinicians to be delusional. In the United Kingdom, Section 3 of the Mental Health Act 1983 allows a person to be detained for treatment when they have a mental disorder and treatment is necessary for their own health or safety or for the protection of others. In Australia, equivalent provisions exist in each state and territory's mental health acts. In the United States, involuntary commitment follows varying state procedures, but consistently applies to individuals judged to be a danger to themselves or others as a result of mental illness.

The critical intersection is the diagnostic standard. Under the DSM-5, a belief is classified as delusional if it is fixed, resistant to counter-evidence, and — the operative clause — "not better explained by another mental disorder, and not accounted for by a widely held cultural or religious belief." This cultural exemption is the fault line.

### 4.2 The Exemption's Structural Problem

The DSM cultural exemption does not evaluate what the belief asserts. It evaluates whether the belief has organisational backing. The American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic bible effectively outsources the determination of epistemic legitimacy to the market share of religious institutions.

Consider the concrete consequence: a psychiatrist assessing a patient who claims to be chosen by God for a divine mission will — under existing diagnostic criteria — evaluate whether this claim is consistent with the patient's "cultural and religious background." If the patient is embedded in a recognised religious community that shares this cosmological framework, the belief is not diagnosable as delusional. If the patient holds the belief in isolation, without institutional affiliation, it may be.

The belief has not changed. The supporting infrastructure has.

Forensic psychiatrists have noted this problem explicitly. A 2020 paper in the *Journal of the American Academy of Psychiatry and the Law* identified the core difficulty: "it would be easy for an individual to find others who share the belief that God can speak to people or that there will be a second coming of the Messiah, but it would be much harder for an individual to find confederates who agree that God has ordained that he is the Messiah." The unshareability criterion — used as a proxy for delusionality — is entirely dependent on whether enough people have already agreed to believe the same thing.

This is circular. A belief is not delusional if enough people share it. But the number of people sharing a belief is a social fact, not an epistemic one. That millions of people believe a claim does not make that claim true, falsifiable, or safe. History is full of widely shared beliefs that were catastrophically wrong. The DSM's cultural exemption essentially codifies an argument from popularity.

### 4.3 The Soviet Precedent and the Danger of Politically Calibrated Psychiatry

The use of psychiatric diagnosis to suppress unwanted beliefs — rather than to treat genuine cognitive dysfunction — has a documented and acknowledged history. Soviet psychiatry under the diagnosis of "sluggish schizophrenia" institutionalised political dissidents whose beliefs, while entirely coherent, were deemed incompatible with the prevailing ideological order. The mechanism was identical to what is described above: the belief is delusional not because of its epistemic content but because it deviates from sanctioned norms.

Western psychiatry has, correctly, condemned this practice as an abuse. The irony is that the DSM's cultural exemption operates on exactly the same structural logic — not to suppress political dissent, but to protect institutionally sanctioned beliefs from the critical scrutiny applied to their unsanctioned equivalents.

The individual who believes he is God's chosen prophet, unsupported by any congregation, risks involuntary detention. The collective that believes it is God's chosen people, supported by a state and a millennia-old institution, is protected by the same law that detains him.

### 4.4 Freedom of Religion vs. the Rights of the Unchosen

A second legal tension runs beneath the psychiatric one. Freedom of religion, as enshrined in international human rights law, protects an individual's right to hold and express religious beliefs. But the rights framework has historically been under-examined with respect to the rights of *those affected by those beliefs* — specifically, the people designated as unchosen.

If a religious doctrine asserts that a particular group is divinely selected and that others are categorically inferior, that doctrine generates third-party harms. The history documented in Section 3 of this paper makes clear that the harms are not hypothetical. They are mass casualty events.

The ICCPR's freedom of religion clause contains a built-in limitation: freedom to manifest one's religion "may be subject only to such limitations as are prescribed by law and are necessary to protect public safety, order, health, or morals or the fundamental rights and freedoms of others." This limitation clause provides a legal basis for distinguishing between religious belief (protected) and religious doctrine that actively generates violence against designated outgroups (regulable).

The problem is that this distinction has never been systematically applied to divine chosenness doctrine as a class. Individual acts of religiously motivated violence are prosecuted. The doctrine that generates and sanctifies them is not examined.

## 5. Why the Doctrine Persists: The Political Economy of Chosenness

Divine chosenness has survived not despite its logical vulnerabilities but partly because of them. Several structural factors explain its persistence.

**Social cohesion function.** Chosenness is a powerful group-binding mechanism. Shared identity as the elect creates in-group solidarity, provides purpose, and renders suffering meaningful. Communities under existential threat have historically intensified chosenness narratives precisely because they provide psychological coherence in conditions of crisis.

**Elite legitimation function.** Leaders who can claim divine endorsement — whether as chosen individuals or representatives of a chosen people — acquire authority that is structurally immune to democratic challenge. The history of theocracy is essentially the history of chosenness claims in the hands of the powerful.

**Unfalsifiability as advantage.** Because the claim of divine selection cannot be empirically tested, it is resistant to the ordinary processes by which false beliefs are corrected. This makes it ideologically durable in a way that empirically testable claims are not. It cannot be disproved. This is not a feature. It is its most dangerous property.

**Legal protection.** As analysed above, existing legal and psychiatric frameworks actively shield the doctrine from critical examination. Religious freedom law, correctly applied to protect sincere personal faith, has been extended — without examination — to cover doctrines with demonstrable histories of generating mass violence.

**Cultural inertia.** Divine chosenness is woven into the founding narratives of most major civilisations. To challenge it is perceived as attacking the foundations of identity, culture, and community. This perception is wielded — often by those with interests in maintaining the doctrine's protective function — to silence critical analysis as bigotry or hatred.

## 6. The Plague Argument: A Formal Case

A pathogen, in the biomedical sense, is an agent that enters a host system, reproduces, and damages the system's functioning. A civilisational pathogen operates analogously: it is a self-replicating idea that, once embedded in a social system, degrades that system's capacity for equitable functioning and generates predictable categories of harm.

Divine chosenness satisfies the criteria for a civilisational pathogen of the first order.

**Self-replication.** The doctrine is transmitted culturally across generations with high fidelity. Religious education, scripture, liturgy, and law encode it into communities and reproduce it reliably. It does not require continuous reinvention. It is, in Boghossian's phrase, sustained by "early childhood indoctrination as the prime infection strategy."

**Host modification.** Communities infected with divine chosenness doctrine undergo measurable modification of their moral reasoning. They become more likely to attribute suffering of outgroups to divine justice, more likely to frame violence against outgroups as sanctioned or required, and less likely to subject their own group's actions to the same moral standards applied to others. This is not a speculative claim. It is the documented pattern of every case study in Section 3.

**Systemic damage.** The damage inflicted by the doctrine is not incidental to its content. It is generative of it. The doctrine's core assertion — that one group is cosmically superior and mandated — is not separable from its consequences. The violence of colonialism, apartheid, ethnic cleansing, and theocratic conquest is not a misapplication of divine chosenness. It is its logical extension in conditions of power.

**Resistance to treatment.** Like a well-adapted pathogen, divine chosenness resists the normal corrective mechanisms of civilisation: democratic challenge, legal accountability, scientific scrutiny, and moral argument. Its unfalsifiability is its defence mechanism. This is why, unlike most other harmful ideologies, it has survived across three millennia without significant structural modification.

**Cross-host transmission.** The doctrine is not specific to any single religion or culture. As documented above, it has infected Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, and secular nationalisms. Its host range is universal. Every culture that encounters it can be colonised by it, generating its own variant of the same underlying pattern: God chose us, they are unchosen, therefore our dominion over them is ordained.

The historian Michael Coogan's formulation captures the mechanism precisely: "From antiquity to the present, the idea of being divinely chosen has had powerful and often pernicious effects. If only one group has been divinely chosen then others have not been, and that justifies subjugating them and taking their land." This is not a theological argument for or against the existence of God. It is an observation about what the doctrine *does* — consistently, reliably, across millennia.

The plague metaphor is not rhetorical. It is descriptive. Divine chosenness is an idea that has killed at a scale comparable to many infectious diseases, that self-replicates with high fidelity, that resists correction by its structural properties, and that has never been systematically addressed by civilisation's immune systems — law, science, and democratic accountability — because those systems have been persuaded, through the doctrine's own political influence, to look away.

## 7. Conclusion: Toward Epistemic Equality and Doctrinal Accountability

### 7.1 What This Paper Does Not Argue

Before stating its conclusions, this paper restates what it does not claim.

It does not argue that religious belief is inherently pathological or that individuals who hold religious faith are mentally unwell. The vast majority of religious believers live peaceful, morally coherent lives, and religion provides genuine psychological, communal, and ethical resources. The target of this paper is a specific structural feature of certain doctrines — the assertion of divinely mandated group superiority over designated outgroups — not the experience of faith itself.

It does not argue that any single religion is uniquely guilty. As the historical survey makes clear, the doctrine of divine chosenness has been adopted by every major religious tradition and by many secular nationalist movements. The problem is not Jewish chosenness, or Christian providentialism, or Islamic divine mandate in isolation. The problem is the underlying structural logic that all of these instantiate.

It does not argue that all individuals claiming divine selection should be forcibly medicated. It argues the opposite.

### 7.2 What the Evidence Requires

The evidence assembled in this paper demands three categories of response.

**First, epistemic honesty.** The most immediate requirement is an honest acknowledgment that the current legal and psychiatric treatment of divine chosenness claims is structurally incoherent. A belief is not epistemically validated by its institutional backing. The DSM cultural exemption, as it currently operates, protects not sincere personal faith but the historical market dominance of established religious institutions. This exemption should be revised to apply consistently — either the subjective experience of divine selection is a matter of protected personal belief for all individuals regardless of institutional affiliation, or it is subject to the same clinical scrutiny for all. The present asymmetry — coercive toward the unaffiliated, permissive toward the institutionalised — is legally and ethically indefensible.

**Second, doctrinal accountability.** Freedom of religion, properly understood, protects the right to believe and to worship. It has never been understood, in its original liberal formulation, as a blanket immunity for doctrines that generate systematic violence against outgroups. The ICCPR's limitation clause provides a legal basis for distinguishing between private religious belief (absolutely protected) and public religious doctrine that has a documented history of generating and legitimising violence against designated others (subject to proportionate regulation and scrutiny). No democracy would protect a secular political ideology with an equivalent body count from democratic critique. The application of this principle to religious doctrine is overdue.

**Third, intellectual courage.** The most resistant barrier to confronting divine chosenness is not legal but social. Critical analysis of the doctrine is consistently deflected by accusations of religious bigotry. This deflection is itself a product of the doctrine's political influence. An idea that has generated the Trail of Tears, the Crusades, the apartheid system, and the colonial destruction of indigenous cultures across five continents is not above criticism. Treating it as such is not tolerance. It is complicity.

### 7.3 The Individual and the Institutional

The opening paradox of this paper — an individual who claims divine selection is medicated, while institutions making the same claim on behalf of millions are legally protected — illuminates something deeper than a procedural inconsistency in psychiatric diagnosis.

It reveals that modern liberal democracy has not, in fact, resolved the relationship between power and epistemology. It has merely codified the epistemological preferences of the powerful. Beliefs held by institutionalised majorities are protected. Beliefs held by isolated individuals, particularly those already marginalised by poverty, illness, or social exclusion, are pathologised.

The content of the belief is irrelevant to this determination. What matters is whether the believer has sufficient allies.

This is an ancient problem. John Stuart Mill identified the tyranny of the majority as the core threat to genuine intellectual freedom. Civil authorities who claimed divine revelation, Mill noted, were "actually relied on messages from fellow mortals." The scale of consensus does not manufacture truth. It merely manufactures power.

A genuinely liberal epistemic framework would apply consistent standards: beliefs would be evaluated by their content, their consequences, and their openness to revision — not by the institutional weight of their proponents. Under such a framework, divine chosenness doctrine — across all of its manifestations — would receive the critical scrutiny its three-thousand-year record of downstream violence plainly warrants.

### 7.4 The Prognosis

Divine chosenness will not be eliminated. It is too deeply embedded in too many traditions. But civilisations have managed other persistent doctrinal pathogens — divine right of kings, racial hierarchy, the inferiority of women — through sustained critical analysis, legal reform, and cultural transformation. None of these processes was rapid or complete. All of them were resisted by those whose power the doctrines sustained.

The process of confronting divine chosenness as a class of doctrine — rather than managing its individual violent expressions after the fact — is not an attack on religion. It is an extension of the Enlightenment project: the application of honest scrutiny to claims of authority, wherever they originate, in the interest of a world in which fewer people die because someone else believed they were chosen.

The plague has gone on long enough.
