# The Nuclear Premium: Extended Analysis
## Five Structural Dimensions of Western Nuclear Power's Cost Crisis

**A Companion Research Paper**

---

## Preface

The first paper in this series established the core thesis: that the cost crisis afflicting Western nuclear power is not a property of nuclear technology itself, but of the contractor-subcontractor procurement model, the CWIP financial mechanism, and the absence of institutional continuity that characterises government-managed and state-champion programs in South Korea and China. This companion paper extends that analysis across five additional dimensions:

- **Part I** — The Small Modular Reactor Illusion: how the promise of cheaper nuclear through miniaturisation has reproduced the same structural failures at smaller scale
- **Part II** — The Defence Procurement Parallel: how the F-35 program demonstrates that the nuclear cost disease is a symptom of a broader Western contracting pathology
- **Part III** — The Skills Atrophy Crisis: how thirty years of nuclear inactivity destroyed the workforce and supply chain that made early nuclear cheap
- **Part IV** — Economic Modelling: what Vogtle should have cost, and what the premium represents in real consumer dollars
- **Part V** — Policy Recommendations: what a functional Western nuclear program would look like, modelled on the institutional evidence

These five sections are written as independent analyses and converged in a final synthesis that draws the full structural argument together.

# PART I: The Small Modular Reactor Illusion

## The Promise

When the cost catastrophes of Vogtle and V.C. Summer became undeniable, the nuclear industry's response was not to interrogate the procurement and governance model that had produced them. It was to propose a new generation of smaller reactors — Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) — that would supposedly solve the cost problem through a different mechanism: factory fabrication, standardisation, and economies of series production.

The argument was superficially compelling. Large gigawatt-scale reactors are complex, site-specific, first-of-a-kind projects. SMRs, by contrast, would be manufactured in controlled factory environments, shipped to sites, and assembled with minimal field labour. The parallel drawn was to aircraft manufacture or submarine construction — industries that produce complex, safety-critical machinery at stable costs through industrial standardisation.

The United States Department of Energy backed this vision with over $600 million in funding to NuScale Power, the first company to receive NRC design approval for an SMR. NuScale's Carbon Free Power Project (CFPP) — six 77 MWe modules at the Idaho National Laboratory — was positioned as the proof-of-concept that would demonstrate the SMR pathway to affordable nuclear.

## The Collapse

The project was cancelled in November 2023. NuScale and the Utah Associated Municipal Power Systems (UAMPS) mutually terminated the CFPP after costs escalated from an original estimate of approximately $3.6 billion to $9.3 billion for the full project — a 158% increase before a single module had been fabricated for commercial deployment (Wikipedia, NuScale Power, 2025). The target cost of electricity had risen from a promised $55 per MWh to $89 per MWh, an increase of 62% (E&E News, 2023). Several UAMPS member utilities withdrew from the project when they could no longer justify the projected electricity price to their ratepayers.

Each of the six 77 MW SMR modules was implicitly priced at approximately $1.5 billion apiece — a per-unit cost that any serious analysis would recognise as commercially catastrophic. A $1.5 billion reactor producing 77 megawatts represents a capital cost of roughly $19,500/kW — exceeding even the worst performance of the Vogtle AP1000 (Neutron Bytes, 2023).

The cancellation sent NuScale's share price down 20% in a single session. The company subsequently laid off more than 40% of its workforce in early 2024 (Motley Fool, 2025).

## Why SMRs Reproduced the Same Failure

The NuScale collapse is instructive not because SMR technology is inherently unworkable — the US Navy has operated compact nuclear reactors safely and reliably for seven decades — but because the NuScale project reproduced every structural failure of the large-reactor commercial model in miniature.

The cost escalation at NuScale was not caused by a design flaw or a safety incident. It was caused by the same factors that drive cost escalation in large-scale nuclear: first-of-a-kind construction with no established manufacturing supply chain, regulatory compliance costs that do not scale proportionally with reactor size, project management layers adding overhead without adding value, and the absence of the volume production commitments that would justify the capital investment in factory fabrication infrastructure.

The key insight missed by SMR proponents is that the economies of factory fabrication only materialise at industrial scale. Building six modules is not an industrial program. Building six hundred modules might be. The Korean APR1400 achieves $2,200/kW not because it is small but because it is built repeatedly, by the same integrated organisation, with a continuous workforce and a mature supply chain. The Navy achieves low-cost reactor construction not because its reactors are cheap to build individually, but because it builds them continuously, with stable technical authority and a workforce that accumulates and retains expertise across decades.

SMRs promised to solve nuclear's cost problem through a different physical format while leaving the institutional model entirely unchanged. They offered factory economics without the factory, volume savings without the volume, and standardisation benefits without a committed standardised program. The NuScale failure was not a failure of the SMR concept — it was a failure of the Western institutional model applied to a new reactor format with the same predictable result.

It is worth noting the contrast with the navy's track record. The government-owned, contractor-operated Bettis and Knolls Atomic Power Laboratories — research and engineering facilities devoted solely to naval nuclear propulsion work — have continuously developed and refined compact reactor designs for decades (DOE, 2026). The S8G reactor plant powering Ohio-class submarines is a compact, high-output naval reactor built to exacting standards at predictable cost. The difference between the Navy's compact reactors and NuScale's commercial SMRs is not the physics — it is the institutional model.

## The Lesson of Part I

The SMR story extends and reinforces the central thesis of the companion paper. Changing the size of the reactor does not change the structural pathologies of the procurement model. As long as Western nuclear development is characterised by first-of-a-kind projects, fragmented contractor chains, no institutional continuity, and financial mechanisms that insulate decision-makers from the consequences of failure, the cost trajectory will be the same regardless of the reactor format.

The NuScale failure is not the end of the SMR story — advanced reactor concepts continue to attract investment and policy attention. But it is a definitive demonstration that cheaper nuclear cannot be achieved by changing the shape of the reactor while leaving unchanged the system within which it is built.

# PART II: The Defence Procurement Parallel

## The F-35 and the Anatomy of a Western Procurement Disease

The Western nuclear cost crisis does not exist in isolation. It is the most visible manifestation of a broader pathology that afflicts the entire Western defence and strategic infrastructure procurement system. Understanding the structural parallels between nuclear construction and defence procurement — particularly the F-35 Joint Strike Fighter program — reveals that the problem is not specific to nuclear technology or the nuclear industry. It is a systemic feature of how Western governments procure complex, long-duration, high-stakes technical projects through contractor-dependent models.

## What the F-35 Program Promised

The F-35 Joint Strike Fighter was conceived in the 1990s as the answer to a specific procurement logic: rather than developing separate next-generation fighters for the Air Force, Navy, and Marines — three different aircraft with three separate development programs — a single common platform would serve all three services with minor modifications. The stated objective was affordability. Lockheed Martin, the prime contractor, said the objective was to develop an aircraft for "half of what today's airplanes cost" (Fortune/Yahoo Finance, 2023). The original baseline cost estimate for the entire program was $233 billion in 2001 (CAGW, 2025).

## What it Actually Cost

Total acquisition costs have exceeded $428 billion, or 84 percent greater than the initial estimate of $233 billion (CAGW, 2025). As of December 2023, the most recently available estimate comes in at approximately $485 billion — more than double the original estimate (National Defense Magazine, 2025). Lifecycle costs — including operation and sustainment over the F-35's 77-year service life — are estimated at $1.58 trillion, bringing the total acquisition and sustainment cost to over $2 trillion (National Defense Magazine, 2025).

The F-35 program has been in continuous development since 2001. It is the most expensive weapons program in human history. It has been plagued, in the GAO's own repeated language, by "routine cost growth, production overruns and other shortcomings." As one congressional watchdog summarised: "In our reviews of the F-35 program over the last 2 decades, we have routinely recounted cost and schedule overruns and other shortfalls" (GAO, 2025).

The Block 4 modernisation subprogram — a $16.5 billion effort to upgrade the F-35's hardware and software — is now more than $6 billion over its original estimates and five years behind schedule (Stars and Stripes, 2025). The GAO found that the program office was paying incentives to contractors even though production deadlines had not been met. The F-35 program's total procurement costs have increased by $13.4 billion since the last cost estimate in 2019 alone (GAO, 2023).

Contractors — Lockheed Martin and Pratt & Whitney — have repeatedly delivered aircraft and engines behind schedule and falling short of contract specifications. Hundreds of major variance requests (MVRs) have been issued, and the program office lacks the adequate mechanisms to track them (National Defense Magazine, 2025).

## The Structural Isomorphism with Nuclear

The parallel between the F-35 program and Western nuclear construction is not superficial. Both programs share an identical structural DNA:

**Prime contractor dependency with no government technical authority.** In both programs, the government client has progressively ceded technical design authority to the prime contractor. Lockheed Martin controls the F-35 design. Westinghouse controlled the AP1000 design. In both cases, the government client — the Pentagon or the utility — has limited ability to independently verify the contractor's technical claims, schedule assessments, or cost projections. The information asymmetry between the government client and the contractor is structural, not incidental.

**Concurrency: building before designing.** A defining feature of the F-35 failure — criticised repeatedly by the GAO — was "concurrency": beginning production before development was complete, so that design changes had to be retrofitted into already-manufactured aircraft at enormous cost. This is precisely what happened at Vogtle: construction began before the AP1000 design was 100% complete, leading to massive volumes of design changes and rework (Nuclear Engineering International, 2026).

**Cost-plus incentive structures that reward complexity and delay.** Both programs feature contracting arrangements in which the contractor is reimbursed for costs incurred plus a fee. In nuclear, CWIP enables this cost-plus dynamic at the utility level — every dollar added to the rate base earns a guaranteed return. In defence, cost-plus contracts mean that delays and overruns increase, rather than decrease, contractor revenue. The risk of failure falls on the government client (and ultimately the taxpayer) while the contractor is protected.

**Too-big-to-fail lock-in.** Once the F-35 program was sufficiently advanced, there was no realistic alternative strike fighter for the US military. Once Vogtle was 50% complete, Georgia Power had no realistic option but to continue — the sunk cost had become an argument for further spending. In both cases, contractors and vendors benefit from the progressive elimination of the client's exit option.

**The absence of an alternative model comparator.** The US government has not built a comparable combat aircraft under a government-managed, vertically integrated model in the post-Cold War era. It therefore has no contemporary baseline for what a fighter aircraft should cost. Similarly, the gap in US commercial nuclear construction since the 1970s means there is no contemporary government-managed comparator for what a civilian power reactor should cost.

## What Alternative Models Demonstrate

The contrast with programs structured differently is illuminating. The Navy's shipbuilding program — while far from perfect — operates with considerably more government technical authority than either the F-35 or nuclear programs. Naval Reactors maintains government technical control over reactor design, with contractor involvement strictly managed and with government engineers retaining independent design verification capability. The result, as documented in the companion paper, is a continuous, low-cost, zero-accident reactor construction and operation record spanning seven decades.

The KEPCO model in South Korea similarly demonstrates what happens when the government retains technical authority rather than delegating it entirely to contractors. KEPCO Engineering & Construction retains the design function. Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power retains construction management. Doosan Enerbility manufactures major components within an integrated supply chain. The government retains oversight authority across the entire value chain. There is no multi-layer subcontracting stack interposed between the client's technical requirements and the organisation responsible for delivering them.

## The Lesson of Part II

The F-35 program and the nuclear construction cost crisis are manifestations of the same institutional disease: the progressive transfer of technical authority, design control, and risk from government clients to private contractors, combined with contracting structures that do not make contractors bear the full financial consequences of failure.

This is not an argument against private sector involvement in complex technical programs. It is an argument that the specific model of contracting — cost-plus, concurrent, with information asymmetry and no government technical authority — produces predictable failure regardless of the domain in which it is applied. Fighter aircraft, nuclear power plants, and SMRs alike have been subjected to this model and produced the same results.

The alternative — where the government or a state-backed champion retains technical authority and the contractor is managed as a disciplined supplier rather than treated as an autonomous technical authority — consistently produces better outcomes. Rickover's management of Westinghouse in the naval program is the template: Westinghouse built the reactors, but Rickover's Naval Reactors organisation retained independent technical authority to verify every claim Westinghouse made. That adversarial oversight — the government as informed, technically capable client — is what is entirely absent from both the F-35 program and modern commercial nuclear construction.

# PART III: The Skills Atrophy Crisis

## The Thirty-Year Gap and Its Consequences

One of the less-discussed contributors to Western nuclear's cost crisis is the most concrete: the destruction of the skilled workforce, supply chain, and institutional knowledge base that made early nuclear construction achievable, through three decades of near-complete inactivity.

From the early 1970s onwards, no new nuclear power plants were ordered in the United States. The Three Mile Island accident in 1979 accelerated a process already underway. By the 1980s and 1990s, nuclear construction had effectively ceased. When the AP1000 program began at Vogtle and V.C. Summer in 2009, it did so into a construction industry that had not built a nuclear power plant in thirty years.

## The Workforce That Was Lost

The nuclear workforce of the 1960s and 1970s was built over the previous two decades. It comprised not only nuclear engineers and reactor physicists, but an entire ecosystem of skilled tradespeople: welders qualified to nuclear standards, pipefitters with nuclear-grade certification, ironworkers experienced with nuclear-grade concrete formwork, instrumentation and control technicians familiar with nuclear safety systems, and — crucially — a mid-career cohort of project managers and construction supervisors who had actual experience building nuclear plants.

That workforce was largely gone by 2009. Nearly 40% of the nuclear energy workforce is expected to retire within the next decade, and the industry already faces a critical shortage of mid-career professionals (DOE, cited in Amerit Consulting, 2025). The Nuclear Energy Institute's workforce surveys found a bimodal age distribution — older workers approaching retirement, newer graduates entering the field, and a critical gap in the 10-to-15-year experience range that produces first- and second-level management (NIH/NCBI, 2021).

The skills gap extends well beyond graduate-level nuclear engineers. One industry analysis identified a critical shortage of nuclear-qualified machinists, welders, inspectors, nondestructive examination specialists, and project managers (ANS, 2026). Of the approximately 67,000 welders in the Australian workforce at the time of a recent study, fewer than 5,000 possessed the expertise to weld to the highest standards required for nuclear power plants (World Nuclear Report, 2024) — and Australia is considering building nuclear power. The US situation is not materially better relative to the scale of ambition.

According to the US Department of Energy, the nuclear energy workforce is trending older, with 60% of workers between the ages of 30 and 54, and 23% fewer workers under the age of 30 compared to the overall energy workforce (Astute People, 2025). The number of nuclear engineering graduates has been declining — down approximately 25% since 2012 — and nuclear makes up only a small percentage of total engineering graduates even then (AEG, 2026).

## The Supply Chain That Was Lost

The workforce problem is matched by an equally serious supply chain problem. Nuclear construction requires components manufactured to standards that most commercial suppliers cannot meet — nuclear-grade steel forgings, reactor pressure vessels, steam generators, reactor coolant pumps, and complex instrumentation systems all require specialised manufacturing capability that must be maintained through continuous demand.

When US nuclear construction stopped in the 1970s, the domestic nuclear manufacturing supply chain gradually atrophied. By 2009, there was no domestic manufacturer capable of producing large nuclear forgings — the United States was entirely dependent on Japan Steel Works, which had a global monopoly on the largest nuclear-grade forgings, producing only four to six reactor pressure vessel heads per year for the entire world market (a constraint documented in multiple nuclear industry analyses).

The "chicken-and-egg" problem identified in recent supply chain analyses is structurally inevitable: suppliers hesitate to expand capacity and develop the supply chain without assured demand, while customers hesitate to place orders without a developed supply chain in place (ANS, 2026). This deadlock creates significant market paralysis that is not resolvable by individual project decisions — it requires a sustained, committed program at the national level to justify the capital investment in supply chain restoration.

The Hinkley Point C Managing Director described the UK version of this problem with unusual frankness: restarting the nuclear construction industry in Britain after a twenty-year pause has been hard, and relearning nuclear skills, creating a new supply chain, and training a workforce is an immense task (World Nuclear Report, 2024). That statement, from the project's own management, is an admission that the cost overruns at Hinkley are substantially attributable to the workforce and supply chain destruction that resulted from the twenty-year gap — and that the costs of rebuilding those capabilities are being borne entirely by the ratepayers of a single project.

## The Knowledge That Was Lost

Beyond physical skills and manufacturing capacity, the thirty-year gap destroyed something harder to quantify and harder to rebuild: the institutional knowledge embedded in the organisations that built nuclear plants. Project management knowledge — how to sequence activities, how to manage multi-thousand-person construction sites, how to resolve design conflicts between nuclear safety requirements and construction practicalities — is not contained in textbooks. It is contained in experienced people who learned it by doing it.

When those people retired, the knowledge retired with them. The younger engineers who replaced them had academic training but no experiential knowledge. The construction managers who ran Vogtle had never managed a nuclear construction project. The quality assurance inspectors had never seen the problems they were being asked to catch.

This knowledge destruction has a direct and quantifiable cost. The MIT Eash-Gates study found that on-site labour productivity at recent US nuclear plants is up to thirteen times lower than industry expectations. A significant portion of that productivity collapse is attributable to the absence of experienced supervision — people who know what "correct" looks like and can identify problems before they become rework.

South Korea avoided this problem precisely because KEPCO and KHNP maintained a continuous construction program. Engineers who joined the program in the 1980s and 1990s were still working when the Barakah reactors were being built in the 2010s. The institutional knowledge of how to build an APR1400 was not an abstraction — it was in the heads and hands of the people actually building it.

## The Lesson of Part III

The skills atrophy crisis is not an independent cause of Western nuclear's cost problem — it is a downstream consequence of the institutional model. A government-managed nuclear program, or a state-champion model like KEPCO, does not allow skills and supply chain capacity to atrophy because it maintains continuous activity. The US Navy has not experienced skills atrophy in its reactor program because it has never stopped building reactors. South Korea has not experienced skills atrophy in its nuclear construction workforce because it has never stopped building reactors.

The commercial model in the United States, driven by utility economics and interest rates rather than national energy strategy, stopped building reactors when they became commercially unviable in the 1970s. The three-decade gap that followed destroyed the very capabilities needed to restart the program, and the cost of rebuilding those capabilities — in workforce training, supply chain development, and institutional knowledge reconstruction — is now being charged, through CWIP and electricity rate increases, to the ratepayers of the first projects attempting the restart.

The reconstruction report cited in the ANS analysis explicitly references the initial buildout of the nuclear workforce in the 1950s and 1960s as the potential template for future efforts — calling for dramatic investments in centralised and standardised training at the local, state, and federal levels (ANS, 2026). The lesson is clear: the workforce and supply chain that made early nuclear cheap were products of deliberate, sustained government investment in institutional capability. They did not emerge spontaneously from market forces and they cannot be rebuilt by market forces alone.

# PART IV: Economic Modelling

## What Vogtle Should Have Cost — and What the Premium Represents

The abstract arguments about institutional models and procurement structures become concrete when translated into dollars. This section attempts to quantify the "nuclear premium" — the excess cost of the contractor-dependent Western model relative to the best available alternative — and to express that premium in terms that reveal its full significance for energy consumers.

## The Baseline: What South Korea Demonstrates

The most reliable contemporary benchmark for what a large light-water reactor of current-generation design should cost to build is the South Korean domestic overnight capital cost of approximately $2,200 per kilowatt (IEA/NEA, 2020; Latitude Media, 2025). This figure is not a theoretical projection or a planning estimate — it is the realised cost of actually building and completing APR1400 reactors in South Korea under current conditions, with current labour costs, current regulatory standards, and current safety requirements.

The APR1400 and the AP1000 are not identical designs, but they are the same generation of pressurised water reactor technology. Both are large light-water reactors with passive safety features and conventional construction techniques. The physics, the materials, the safety margins, and the regulatory objectives are broadly comparable.

Using the South Korean benchmark as the reference point for what nuclear construction should cost, we can calculate the excess cost — the "nuclear premium" — embedded in the Western contractor model.

## The Vogtle Premium

Vogtle Units 3 and 4 produced approximately 2,234 MWe of generating capacity at a total project cost of approximately $35 billion (World Nuclear Association, 2026). The implied overnight capital cost is approximately $15,650/kW when the total project cost is divided by capacity.

At the South Korean benchmark of $2,200/kW, the same 2,234 MWe of generating capacity would cost approximately $4.9 billion.

The excess cost — the nuclear premium for choosing the Western contractor model over the South Korean state-champion model — is therefore approximately $30 billion for a single two-unit project.

To contextualise that figure:
- $30 billion would build approximately 10,000 MW of utility-scale solar at current US costs of approximately $1,000–1,500/kW
- $30 billion would fund the entire annual budget of the US Department of Energy for approximately three years
- $30 billion would build approximately 13 two-unit nuclear plants at South Korean costs
- The average Georgia Power household will pay an estimated $35 per month more on electricity bills as a result of Vogtle — a figure more than double Georgia Power's own original estimate of $15 per month (Georgia Conservation Voters, 2024)
- Georgia Power added $11.1 billion to its rate base for its 45.7% share of the project alone, resulting in a 25% electricity rate increase — the largest in Georgia's history (Power Magazine, 2024)
- Before the new reactors produced a single watt of power, the average Georgia Power household was charged approximately $1,000 in pre-construction financing fees (nuclearcosts.org, 2024)

A 2024 consumer report by six Georgia groups calculated the effective cost to ratepayers at $10,784 per kilowatt — compared to $900–$1,500 per kilowatt for wind, solar, or natural gas (Third Act / Georgia Conservation Voters, 2024). On that basis, Vogtle produced electricity costing between 7 and 12 times more per unit of capacity than the cheapest alternatives available.

## The System-Wide Premium

Vogtle is not an outlier in the Western nuclear cost distribution — it is representative of it. Of the 39 nuclear plants constructed since 2000 with reliable cost data, only Hinkley Point C costs more per megawatt than Vogtle (Dumitriu, 2023). Together, Vogtle and Hinkley Point C represent approximately $71 billion ($35B + £48B) in construction spending for approximately 5,500 MWe of generating capacity — an average of approximately $12,900/kW.

At the South Korean benchmark of $2,200/kW, 5,500 MWe would cost approximately $12.1 billion.

The combined nuclear premium embedded in just these two projects — the excess cost attributable to the Western contractor model — is therefore approximately $59 billion. That is $59 billion extracted from electricity ratepayers in Georgia and the United Kingdom through electricity bills, CWIP charges, and government loan guarantees, over and above what the same generating capacity would have cost under a functional procurement model.

## The VC Summer Counterfactual

The V.C. Summer case adds a particularly stark dimension to this economic analysis. More than $10.4 billion was spent before the project was abandoned with nothing to show for it (Power Magazine, 2021). At South Korean costs, $10.4 billion would have built approximately 4,700 MWe of nuclear generating capacity — roughly two complete two-unit plants.

Instead, South Carolina ratepayers received two abandoned reactor sites, $2 billion in charges billed to households before the cancellation, a utility company driven into acquisition, and four senior executives prosecuted for fraud. The opportunity cost is not merely financial — it is the generating capacity that would have existed, operating cleanly, for the next six decades.

## The NuScale Modelling Comparison

The NuScale project offers a particularly clean comparison because it involved a reactor technology explicitly designed to be cheaper than large-scale nuclear through factory fabrication. The projected cost reached $89/MWh before cancellation (E&E News, 2023).

For comparison:
- South Korean nuclear electricity costs approximately $42/MWh at a 7% discount rate (IEA/NEA, 2020)
- New utility-scale solar in the US costs approximately $30–45/MWh (LCOE)
- Natural gas combined cycle costs approximately $40–60/MWh depending on fuel prices

The NuScale SMR, heralded as the affordable next generation of nuclear, was pricing itself out of the market before it had built a single commercial module — at a cost per unit of $1.5 billion for 77 MW, implying $19,500/kW, a figure that exceeds even Vogtle's extraordinary cost per kilowatt.

## The Lesson of Part IV

The economic modelling reveals three conclusions that should be at the centre of any serious nuclear policy discussion:

First, the excess cost of the Western contractor model relative to the best international benchmark represents tens of billions of dollars per project — costs borne almost entirely by electricity consumers through rate increases, CWIP charges, and bill increases, not by the shareholders or executives of the organisations making the decisions.

Second, the projects that have actually been completed (Vogtle, Hinkley) are still preferable to the projects that were abandoned (V.C. Summer, NuScale Idaho), in the narrow sense that at least they produce electricity. But the scale of the premium suggests that the ratepayers of these projects would have been better served by almost any alternative investment in generating capacity, including renewables, gas, or nuclear built under a different institutional model.

Third, the SMR pathway does not resolve the cost problem as long as the institutional model remains unchanged. NuScale's experience demonstrates that first-of-a-kind small reactor construction under the Western contractor model costs more per kilowatt than large-scale reactor construction under the same model — the economies of small-scale factory fabrication do not materialise without the volume production commitment that only a sustained, government-directed program could provide.

# PART V: Policy Recommendations

## Building a Western Nuclear Industry That Works

The evidence assembled across the companion paper and this extended analysis points clearly toward a set of policy interventions. These are not speculative — they are largely deductions from programs that already work, applied to the institutional context of Western democracies. The model exists. South Korea has proven it. China has proven it. The US Navy has proven it. The question is whether Western governments have the institutional will to adopt it for civilian nuclear power.

## Recommendation 1: Establish a National Nuclear Authority with Government Technical Capability

The single most important reform is the creation of a government entity that retains independent technical authority over nuclear design, construction, and operation — not as a regulator (the NRC already performs that function) but as a technically capable client that can independently verify contractor claims, manage project schedules, and make engineering decisions without dependence on contractor-supplied information.

This is precisely what Naval Reactors does for the Navy's nuclear program. It is what KEPCO Engineering & Construction does within the South Korean nuclear ecosystem. It is what the Atomic Energy Commission's national laboratories did in the 1950s and 1960s.

The institutional architecture of such a body already partially exists. The Department of Energy's national laboratories — Idaho, Argonne, Oak Ridge, Brookhaven — retain nuclear engineering expertise that could form the technical core of a National Nuclear Authority. What is currently missing is the mandate and the authority: the right to serve as the technically capable owner-operator on major nuclear construction projects, rather than as a research and advisory function.

South Korea's government has, as recently as May 2026, strengthened its own version of this model — consolidating nuclear export management under KEPCO with government oversight authority, requiring prior consultation on key decisions during overseas nuclear projects, and establishing a Nuclear Export Promotion Act to provide the legal framework for government technical and financial authority (Seoul Economic Daily, 2026). Western governments should study this model closely.

## Recommendation 2: Mandate Continuous Construction Programs, Not Isolated Projects

The skills atrophy crisis documented in Part III is a direct consequence of treating nuclear construction as a series of isolated, one-off projects rather than as a continuous industrial program. The solution is to commit, at the national level, to a program of sequential reactor construction that maintains workforce continuity, supply chain investment, and institutional learning across successive projects.

South Korea's domestic nuclear program is the proof of concept. By building reactors in a continuous sequence — not one project followed by a decade of inactivity, but successive projects with overlapping construction timelines — the Korean program has maintained the skilled workforce and supply chain that make $2,200/kW costs achievable.

A US or UK national program of this type would require government commitment to a minimum construction rate — the equivalent of one or two reactor units beginning construction per year for a sustained period. This is not unprecedented: in the peak years of the US nuclear construction program in the late 1960s and early 1970s, dozens of reactors were ordered simultaneously. The Navy has built approximately one nuclear reactor plant per year since the 1950s without interruption.

The NSI supply chain report's call for "dramatic investments" in centralised and standardised training and education at the local, state, and federal levels (ANS, 2026) is the workforce dimension of this recommendation. A sustained construction program creates the demand signal that justifies those training investments; the training investments create the workforce that makes the program viable. The two are mutually reinforcing — but only if the program commitment comes first.

## Recommendation 3: Replace CWIP with Performance-Linked Finance

The Construction Work in Progress accounting mechanism, as documented in the companion paper, systematically inverts the incentive structure of nuclear construction by making cost overruns profitable for utilities and transferring risk entirely to ratepayers.

The replacement mechanism should link utility returns to construction performance rather than construction spending. A utility that completes a nuclear project on time and on budget should earn a higher rate of return than a utility that delivers the same project late and over budget. This requires regulators to set performance benchmarks at project approval — expected construction cost, expected schedule, expected cost of electricity — and to use those benchmarks to determine what costs are recoverable from ratepayers versus what must be absorbed by utility shareholders.

Virginia's CWIP reform — which excluded 20% of development costs from CWIP eligibility and introduced additional safeguards — represents a step in this direction. But the reform required is more fundamental: the risk-return relationship must be realigned so that utilities have a genuine financial interest in disciplining their contractors rather than a financial interest in accommodating contractor cost growth.

A complementary reform is to require that major nuclear construction contracts be awarded on a fixed-price, design-complete basis — construction beginning only after the design is sufficiently advanced that scope changes during construction are genuinely exceptional rather than routine. The AP1000 disaster at Vogtle began when construction started before the design was 100% complete; that single decision created the design-change-rework cycle that consumed billions in excess cost.

## Recommendation 4: Adopt a State-Champion Model for Nuclear Export and Construction

The KEPCO model — a state-backed but commercially operating champion with integrated design, construction, and operational capabilities — represents the most viable organisational template for Western nuclear revival. Several Western countries have existing state energy companies that could serve as the foundation for such a champion: EDF in France, ENGIE, or a newly constituted national nuclear authority in the United Kingdom or United States.

The key structural features that make KEPCO successful are:

**Vertical integration across the value chain.** KEPCO Engineering & Construction handles design; Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power handles construction and operation; Doosan Enerbility manufactures major components; KEPCO Nuclear Fuel handles fuel; KEPCO KPS handles maintenance. The entire value chain is domestically integrated, with clear lines of accountability between each function (Seoul National University Nuclear Policy Center, 2026). The absence of an arm's-length contractor relationship at each interface eliminates the coordination costs, information asymmetry, and misaligned incentives that characterise the Western model.

**Sustained government commitment to a domestic construction program.** KEPCO's commercial viability as a nuclear champion depends on a domestic construction program that provides the throughput to justify its investments in workforce, supply chain, and institutional capability. Without that commitment, the economics of vertical integration do not work — you cannot justify the cost of maintaining a world-class nuclear engineering organisation on the basis of a single project every decade.

**Government technical oversight without day-to-day management.** The South Korean government has consistently maintained oversight authority over key decisions in KEPCO's nuclear program — including large-scale borrowing, investment, and contract terms — without micromanaging the engineering and commercial details. This is the appropriate division of labour: government as technically capable principal, KEPCO as expert agent, with clear accountability structures linking the two.

## Recommendation 5: Rebuild the Educational and Training Pipeline as a National Priority

The workforce crisis documented in Part III cannot be resolved by the nuclear industry alone. The supply of nuclear-qualified engineers, project managers, welders, and instrumentation specialists is a public good — it benefits the entire energy system, the defence industrial base (which depends on the same nuclear expertise), and the country's long-term energy security. Its provision cannot be left entirely to market signals from an industry that has been economically marginal for decades.

The 1950s and 1960s buildout of the nuclear workforce cited by the NSI report as a model was a deliberate national investment — funded through the AEC, executed through national laboratories and universities, and sustained by the consistent demand signal of a government-directed construction program (ANS, 2026). Replicating that buildout requires the same combination of elements: government funding for training programs, university nuclear engineering departments, and apprenticeship programs in nuclear-qualified trades, sustained by a government commitment to a construction program that creates the jobs those graduates and apprentices will fill.

Several institutions already provide the foundation for this: the 34 US universities with nuclear engineering programs, the DOE national laboratories, the Navy's Nuclear Power School, and the vocational training programs associated with existing nuclear plants. What is missing is the scale of investment and the coordination that a national program would provide.

## Convergence: The Full Structural Argument

The five parts of this extended paper, read together with the companion paper, converge on a single structural argument:

Western nuclear power is expensive not because of the physics, the fuel, or the safety requirements. It is expensive because of a cascade of institutional failures that have compounded over fifty years:

The **contractor model** eliminated government technical authority and institutional continuity, replacing them with fragmented, incentive-misaligned procurement chains that produce low productivity, high overhead, and zero institutional learning transfer between projects.

The **CWIP mechanism** eliminated the financial discipline that would otherwise force utilities and contractors to manage costs, by transferring the consequences of failure from decision-makers to ratepayers.

The **thirty-year construction gap** destroyed the workforce and supply chain that made early nuclear cheap, ensuring that every new project begins with the cost penalties of a first-of-a-kind construction.

The **SMR false promise** offered a technological solution to an institutional problem, reproducing the same failures at smaller scale without addressing their root cause.

The **defence procurement parallel** reveals that nuclear construction is not uniquely dysfunctional — it is an instance of a broader Western contracting pathology that produces the same cost disease in every domain where it is applied.

Against this backdrop, the path to affordable nuclear is not another new reactor design, another government loan guarantee, or another regulatory streamlining exercise. It is the restoration of the institutional conditions — government technical authority, integrated state-champion organisations, continuous construction programs, performance-linked finance, and sustained workforce investment — that made nuclear cheap the first time.

The United States built more than fifty reactors in fifteen years. Admiral Rickover built the nuclear Navy in sixteen. South Korea builds reactors at $2,200/kW today. These are not historical accidents or geopolitical coincidences. They are the outputs of institutional models that work.

The models are known. The evidence is clear. The cost of continuing with the current model is documented, auditable, and borne by millions of electricity consumers who had no choice in the matter. The question is no longer whether affordable nuclear is possible.

The question is whether Western governments will build it the right way.
