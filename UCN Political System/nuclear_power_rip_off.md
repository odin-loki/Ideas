# The Nuclear Premium: How the Contractor-Subcontractor Model Turned the World's Most Efficient Energy Source into the Most Expensive

**A Research Paper on the Structural Cost Crisis in Western Commercial Nuclear Power**

---

## Abstract

Nuclear power was not always expensive. Between 1954 and the late 1960s, the United States government built more than fifty nuclear reactors across national laboratory sites and proved — through the Navy's Nuclear Propulsion Program — that reactors could be designed, constructed, and operated safely, on schedule, and at costs that would make electricity generation economically transformative. Today, the same technology routinely costs ten to seventeen times more per kilowatt to build in the United States and United Kingdom than it costs state-managed programs in South Korea and China. This paper argues that the cost crisis in modern Western nuclear power is not primarily a product of regulatory burden, technological complexity, or the inherent difficulty of the engineering — it is a structural artifact of the contractor-subcontractor procurement model, compounded by financial mechanisms that transfer risk from utilities to ratepayers and eliminate incentives for cost discipline. Using the historical record of government-managed American nuclear programs, the catastrophic failures of the AP1000 program at Vogtle and V.C. Summer, the international cost comparisons between South Korea, China, Finland, and the United Kingdom, and the economic mechanics of Construction Work in Progress (CWIP) accounting, this paper makes the case that Western nuclear power has become, in its current form, a structural rip-off — one whose costs reflect a broken procurement and governance model rather than the true cost of producing nuclear electricity.

---

## 1. Introduction: Two Nuclear Industries

There are, in effect, two nuclear power industries operating in the world today. One builds reactors on schedule, at predictable costs, and generates electricity that is competitive with any other source. The other routinely doubles or triples its budget, delays projects by a decade or more, bankrupts its contractors, and charges ratepayers for the privilege of financing failures they did not choose.

The first industry operates in South Korea and China. The second operates in the United States and the United Kingdom.

What separates them is not the physics of fission, which is identical. It is not safety, since the South Korean and Chinese nuclear fleet has an exemplary operational record. It is not even regulatory strictness per se — South Korea's nuclear regulator is no less rigorous than the United States Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC). What separates them is the model by which nuclear power plants are conceived, financed, designed, and built.

In South Korea, nuclear construction is managed by a vertically integrated state champion — Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) — that retains design authority, construction management, and workforce continuity across successive projects. In the United States, a utility corporation contracts a reactor vendor (Westinghouse), which subcontracts a construction manager (CB&I, later Bechtel), which subcontracts hundreds of specialist firms, none of whom bear the full consequences of failure and many of whom have strong structural incentives to extend the project.

This paper traces how the United States arrived at this state, what it cost, and why the evidence suggests the current model represents not an unavoidable feature of nuclear technology but a deliberate — and deeply expensive — institutional choice.

## 2. The Baseline: What Government-Managed Nuclear Actually Achieved

### 2.1 The National Laboratory Era

The cost crisis of modern nuclear power is thrown into sharp relief by the accomplishments of American government-managed nuclear programs in the 1950s and 1960s. In that period, the U.S. established the National Reactor Testing Station in Idaho — a precursor to Idaho National Laboratory — and built more than fifty nuclear reactors. Additional reactors were built at Argonne, Oak Ridge, Los Alamos, Brookhaven, Hanford, and Savannah River, all directly managed by the federal government (Third Way, Advanced Nuclear 101, 2022).

At the Hanford Site along the Columbia River, nine production reactors were constructed between 1944 and 1963. The first eight were built during and immediately after World War II to supply plutonium for the Manhattan Project. The ninth, the N Reactor — a dual-purpose facility producing both weapons-grade plutonium and civilian electricity — was proposed by General Electric in 1953 and broke ground at a ceremony attended by President Kennedy in September 1963. The dual-purpose reactor for Hanford was first proposed by General Electric as a way to launch a civilian nuclear industry, with business advocates seeing it as the entry point into the private nuclear arena (HistoryLink.org, 2019).

At Savannah River in South Carolina, five heavy-water production reactors were built between 1951 and 1955. The first, R Reactor, became operational in December 1953; P, L, K, and C Reactors followed in February, July, October, and February of 1954 and 1955 respectively. These were large, complex facilities that went critical in sequence with remarkable speed — if slow by Hanford standards, this was much faster than later generations of nuclear engineers would be able to achieve (Wikipedia, Savannah River Site, 2024).

The critical point is not merely the speed, but the unit economics. Historical analysis of construction costs for nuclear plants in this era shows overnight construction costs declining sharply from a high of $6,800/kW to a low of $1,300/kW — an 81% reduction — as the learning curve drove improvements across the first phase of demonstration reactors between 1954 and 1968. The subsequent "turnkey contract" era from 1964 to 1967 saw costs stabilise in the range of $1,000–$1,500/kW (Lovering et al., *Energy Policy*, 2016). To contextualise these figures: the Vogtle AP1000 project, completed in 2024, came in at approximately $17,500/kW per unit — more than ten times higher in inflation-adjusted terms than the best costs achieved in the early government-managed era.

### 2.2 Admiral Rickover and the Naval Nuclear Propulsion Program

No figure better illustrates the gap between government-managed and contractor-managed nuclear than Admiral Hyman G. Rickover, known as the "Father of the Nuclear Navy." Rickover directed the original development of naval nuclear propulsion and controlled its operations for three decades as director of the U.S. Naval Reactors office. His influence on the Navy and its warships was of such scope that he "may well go down in history as one of the Navy's most important officers" (Wikipedia, Hyman G. Rickover, 2024).

Rickover's achievement was extraordinary: in sixteen years — between 1946, when he was placed in charge of developing nuclear propulsion for the Navy, and 1962, when the USS Enterprise began its maiden deployment — the Navy went from a theoretical propulsion concept to operating the world's largest nuclear-powered vessel, equipped with eight reactors (USNI News, 2018). Under Rickover's leadership, the first test reactor plant (S1W) started up in 1953. The first nuclear-powered vessel, USS Nautilus, put to sea in 1955.

Rickover's model was the antithesis of the modern contractor-dependent approach. He brought intense program management to bear on the companies and shipyards he worked with, in many cases personally developing their technical roadmaps. The Naval Nuclear Propulsion Program's organisational philosophy emphasised flat command structures, clear lines of authority, and a culture of personal ownership — technical, managerial, and fiscal. The Naval Reactors program, operating within a clearly defined executive mandate, compiled what one congressional testimony described as "an unparalleled record of success" (Bowman, Global Security, 2000).

The program's operational statistics speak for themselves. Nuclear-powered warships have safely steamed over 179 million miles on nuclear power. The program has accumulated over 5,100 reactor-years of operation, compared to approximately 2,400 for the entire U.S. commercial power industry (ANS, 2026). Today, the Navy operates more nuclear reactors than the entire U.S. commercial reactor industry (USNI News, 2018).

Rickover himself understood the implication. One Navy veteran, writing about the early civilian nuclear program, described Rickover's position: he used his prestige to insist that any reactor used to generate electricity in the United States should be built and operated by the U.S. Navy to ensure its safety. He observed that you did say the electricity would be free — so why would Wall Street want to build nuclear plants anyway? (FlaglerLive, 2015).

That question — why Wall Street — turns out to be the crux of the entire cost problem.

### 2.3 Shippingport: The Bridge Between Military and Civilian Nuclear

The Naval Nuclear Propulsion Program launched the civilian nuclear power industry. Shippingport Atomic Power Station, which began operations in 1957, was the United States' first full-scale commercial nuclear power plant, derived from naval reactor technology and developed under Rickover's leadership. It demonstrated that nuclear power could reliably produce electricity for civilian use, translating naval propulsion expertise into a peaceful and economically transformative application (ANS, 2026).

Shippingport's total cost was approximately $110 million — close to $1 billion in today's dollars — and it was designed, built, and initially operated under Rickover and Westinghouse Electric Corporation. It was, critically, a government-directed project from conception to commissioning. The institutional discipline that made it work was the same discipline that made the Naval program work: direct government technical authority, Rickover's personal oversight, and a culture that treated nuclear construction as a matter of national engineering pride rather than a financial instrument.

The moment that changed was when the technology was handed to private utilities and their contractor networks, without the institutional scaffolding that had made government nuclear cheap.

## 3. The Great Inversion: How Costs Began to Rise

### 3.1 The Contractor Takeover and the Onset of "Ratcheting"

The transfer of nuclear power development from the government laboratory system and the Navy to private utilities and their engineering contractors began in earnest in the 1960s. Initially, reactor vendors offered turnkey contracts — fixed prices for completed plants — which appeared to contain costs. The turnkey era (1964–1967) saw overnight costs remain in the $1,000–$1,500/kW range. But vendors were absorbing losses on these contracts to build market share, and once turnkey pricing was abandoned, costs began to rise without limit.

From the early to the late 1970s, overnight construction costs in the United States increased from $650 per kilowatt to around $11,000 per kilowatt. Between 1970 and 1978 alone, overnight construction costs increased by up to 200 percent, or 5 to 15 percent annually (IER, 2019).

A significant portion of this increase was driven by what industry insiders called "regulatory ratcheting." Starting in the early 1970s, public concern about nuclear safety — growing in response to accidents and near-misses — led the NRC to continuously tighten construction and equipment standards. Like a ratchet wrench, regulations were constantly tightened, requiring additional equipment and construction labor and materials, but never loosened even when later experience demonstrated that early requirements were unnecessarily stringent (Cohen, *The Nuclear Energy Option*, University of Pittsburgh, 2000).

The material consequences were substantial. Between the early and late 1970s, regulatory requirements increased the quantity of steel needed in a power plant of equivalent electrical output by 41%, the amount of concrete by 27%, the lineal footage of piping by 50%, and the length of electrical cable by 36% (Cohen, 2000). One key indicator — the number of NRC regulatory guides stipulating acceptable design and construction practices — grew almost seven-fold, from 21 in 1971 to 143 in 1978 (Komanoff, *Power Plant Cost Escalation*, 1981, cited in IFP, 2025).

A 1980 study found that increased regulation between the late 1960s and mid-1970s was responsible for a 176% increase in plant cost, and increased labor requirements by 137% (IFP, 2025). But it is critical to note that regulation was not the whole story — nor even the majority of it. The MIT study by Eash-Gates et al. (2020) found that only 30% of the cost increase between 1976 and 1988 could be attributed to increased regulation. The other 70% was attributable to indirect costs — specifically, the explosion of expensive professionals such as engineers, managers, consultants, and construction supervisors layered into the project by the contractor model.

### 3.2 The Productivity Collapse

The most damning finding in the academic literature on nuclear cost escalation is not about regulations or material requirements. It is about labour productivity. The MIT study found that productivity in recent US plants is up to 13 times lower than industry expectations (Eash-Gates et al., *Joule*, 2020). Thirteen times. Not thirteen percent — thirteen times.

This figure deserves to sit at the centre of any analysis of nuclear costs. If a construction workforce is achieving one-thirteenth of the expected output per worker-hour, the cause is not the physics of what is being built. It is the management, organisation, and incentive structure of how the work is being executed.

The reason productivity has collapsed is structural. In the government-managed programs of the 1950s and 1960s, a clear authority — whether the Navy, the AEC, or a national laboratory director — was responsible for every aspect of the project and bore the full consequences of failure. In the modern commercial model, the utility that owns the plant contracts a reactor vendor (e.g., Westinghouse), which contracts construction managers, which in turn contract dozens of specialist subcontractors for civil works, mechanical installation, electrical systems, instrumentation and control, and quality assurance. Each layer adds overhead, coordination costs, and communication latency. Each layer also has different incentives, different risk exposure, and limited accountability for the performance of the layers above and below it.

The Eash-Gates MIT study confirmed this: "soft" factors external to standardised reactor hardware — on-site labour supervision, contractor management layers, coordination overhead — contributed over half of the rapid cost rise from 1976 to 1987 (Eash-Gates et al., 2020). An OECD Nuclear Energy Agency study covering nuclear plant cost estimates from 2010 to 2020 found that increases in indirect costs are the main driver of nuclear plant cost overruns, with labour making up 80% of indirect costs (IFP, 2025). "Indirect costs" is the technical term for the contractor management stack.

The most counterintuitive finding from this literature is this: contrary to expectations, building subsequent plants based on an existing design actually costs more, not less, than building the initial plant (MIT, 2020). This is the inverse of every other complex engineering industry — aerospace, automotive, semiconductor fabrication — where repetition drives learning and cost reduction. In Western nuclear, repetition drives cost *escalation*. The reason is that the contractor-subcontractor model does not transmit institutional learning efficiently. Knowledge accumulated on one project does not flow to the next because the contractors, subcontractors, and workforce composition change. The organisation that builds the second plant does not share the experience of the organisation that built the first.

## 4. Case Studies in Contractor-Driven Failure

### 4.1 Vogtle Units 3 & 4: The Most Expensive Power Plant in American History

The Vogtle nuclear expansion in Waynesboro, Georgia — two AP1000 pressurised water reactors built by Westinghouse and managed by Southern Nuclear — is the defining case study in modern nuclear construction failure. Its cost trajectory is instructive in every detail.

Initial estimates placed the project at $14 billion. Construction began in 2009. By 2024, when Unit 4 finally entered commercial operation — approximately seven years behind schedule — the total cost had reached approximately $35 billion, representing a cost per kilowatt of roughly $17,500/kW per unit and a final project cost more than double the original estimate (World Nuclear Association, 2026). Recent detailed analysis places the figure even higher: one MIT assessment calculated the actual overnight capital cost at $7,956/kW, with total project cost approaching $12,500/kW when financing costs are included, and the final all-in figure for both units at approximately $35–36 billion (MIT CANES, 2024).

The proximate causes of failure have been extensively documented. Westinghouse commenced construction before the AP1000 design was 100% complete, leading to massive volumes of design changes and costly "rework." Prefabricated modules — the central innovation of the AP1000's "modular construction" approach — often arrived at the site incomplete or with incorrect dimensions, requiring expensive and time-consuming on-site modifications (Nuclear Engineering International, 2026). In 2021, the NRC increased its oversight after identifying critical quality control failures at Unit 3: inspectors found over 600 cases where safety-related and non-safety-related cables were not properly separated, a basic regulatory requirement to ensure a single event such as a fire cannot disable redundant safety systems (NEI, 2026).

The supposed advantage of the AP1000 — its modular design — became one of its worst liabilities. The theory was that prefabrication would reduce on-site labour and accelerate construction. The reality was that modules manufactured by inexperienced suppliers in a supply chain that had atrophied during three decades of no nuclear construction in the United States arrived at the site requiring extensive remediation. Westinghouse had no sales of its earlier AP600 design, meaning that neither the vendor, the contractors, nor the workforce had any institutional experience with the modular construction methodology at full scale when Vogtle began.

The financial consequences spread through the entire industry. Westinghouse, financially crippled by losses on both Vogtle and V.C. Summer (see below), filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in March 2017, citing $9 billion in losses from its two U.S. nuclear construction projects (Choose Energy, 2020). Its parent company, Toshiba, suffered losses of extraordinary magnitude. The project was taken over by Southern Nuclear, which managed completion by reverting to more conventional construction practices — at the cost of years of additional time and billions in additional expenditure.

### 4.2 V.C. Summer: The $10.4 Billion Abandonment and the Criminal Aftermath

If Vogtle represents a partial failure — the project at least producing electricity eventually — V.C. Summer in Jenkinsville, South Carolina is a total one. Two AP1000 reactors contracted by SCANA Corporation and Santee Cooper, using the identical Westinghouse design, were begun in 2009 and abandoned in July 2017 with more than $10.4 billion in construction and interest already spent by the owners (Power Magazine, 2021).

What distinguishes V.C. Summer from mere incompetence is the documented pattern of deliberate concealment that followed the project's deterioration. Federal investigations culminating in criminal prosecutions revealed that executives at both SCANA and Westinghouse concealed the true state of the project from investors, ratepayers, legislators, and regulators as costs spiralled and finishing dates receded (Post and Courier, 2023).

Jeffrey Benjamin, former Senior Vice President at Westinghouse, was charged in a sixteen-count federal indictment. The indictment alleged that throughout 2016 and into 2017, when Westinghouse had direct control over the construction and schedule, Benjamin received information that the V.C. Summer units were materially behind schedule and over budget — and that he nevertheless assured the owners the units would be completed on schedule and took active steps to conceal damaging information about the project. During this period, the owners paid Westinghouse over $600 million to construct the two units, both of which were ultimately abandoned (DOJ, 2021).

Kevin Marsh, former SCANA CEO, pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit mail and wire fraud and was sentenced to two years in federal prison. Stephen Byrne, former SCANA executive vice president, also pleaded guilty. Carl Churchman, former Westinghouse vice president and project director for V.C. Summer, pleaded guilty as well.

The ratepayers of South Carolina — who had been paying increased utility bills to fund construction under a state law that allowed utilities to raise rates for construction work in progress — were left holding the bag for $2 billion in charges for reactors that never generated a single watt of electricity (Choose Energy, 2020). The utility SCANA was eventually acquired by Dominion Energy after its share price collapsed.

The V.C. Summer story is not merely a tale of project management failure. It is a story of a structural incentive environment so distorted that rational actors — senior corporate executives — found it preferable to commit federal fraud than to disclose the true cost of the project they had sold to the public. The contractor model, combined with the CWIP financial mechanism (discussed in Section 5), created conditions in which concealment was rational. If the true costs had been disclosed, the project would have been cancelled and the contractors' contracts terminated. As long as concealment held, the money kept flowing.

### 4.3 Olkiluoto 3, Finland: The European Version of the Same Disease

The Vogtle and V.C. Summer failures might be dismissed as uniquely American pathologies — the product of a thirty-year gap in nuclear construction that atrophied the American supply chain and workforce. But the simultaneous failure of the Olkiluoto 3 project in Finland demonstrates that the problem is endemic to the contractor model itself, not to America specifically.

Olkiluoto 3, a 1,600 MWe European Pressurised Reactor (EPR) built by a consortium of Areva and Siemens under a fixed-price turnkey contract with Finnish utility TVO, was contracted in 2003 with a planned completion date of 2009 at a cost of approximately €3 billion. Construction began in 2005. After a series of delays so extended they became notorious, the plant finally reached commercial operation in 2023 — nearly fourteen years behind schedule, at a total cost that has been variously estimated at between €8.5 and €11 billion, representing an overrun of 180–270% (World Nuclear Association, 2024; Carbon Brief, 2016).

The problems began almost immediately. The concrete base slab was laid incorrectly — a structure that is supposed to withstand the weight of the entire plant collapsing on it. Coordination between workers of different nationalities and languages proved deeply problematic. Areva chose primarily French subcontractors rather than Finnish ones, alienating the local workforce and supply chain. Most critically, the lowest bid was selected in the tender process rather than the highest-quality bid, and special requirements for nuclear power plant construction were not adequately emphasised in the contract specifications (Ruuska, cited in European Perspective, 2022).

The legal consequences were byzantine. TVO eventually claimed €2.6 billion in compensation from Areva for the delays. Areva filed a counterclaim of €3.5 billion against TVO, alleging that TVO's "inappropriate behaviour" was responsible for the overruns. The case went to the International Chamber of Commerce's arbitration court in Stockholm. A settlement was eventually reached in 2018 in which Areva and Siemens paid €450 million in compensation to TVO (World Nuclear News, 2018). On a €3 billion contract, not only did Areva not make any profit — it lost approximately €8 billion (European Perspective, 2022). The French state was obliged to bail out Areva, effectively meaning that French taxpayers paid for Finland's nuclear power station cost overruns.

The Olkiluoto 3 experience documents with clinical precision how fixed-price contracting, multi-national subcontracting, inadequate tender specifications, and the absence of integrated project authority produce catastrophic outcomes even when the owner (TVO) is a sophisticated, experienced nuclear utility.

### 4.4 Hinkley Point C, United Kingdom: The World's Most Expensive Building Project

The Hinkley Point C nuclear power station in Somerset, England is the most expensive power plant currently under construction anywhere in the world. It is also, by any reasonable measure, the most expensive building project per unit of generating capacity in human history.

Hinkley Point C was approved in 2016 at an estimated cost of £18 billion (in 2015 prices), with an expected commercial operation date of 2025 for Unit 1. As of 2026, the total construction cost is estimated at £35 billion in 2015 prices — approximately £48 billion in 2026 prices — with Unit 1 now projected to come online no earlier than 2030 (Wikipedia, Hinkley Point C, 2026). The cost has doubled from the original estimate. The delay has lengthened from zero to approximately five years for Unit 1 alone, with the project still in progress.

At approximately £48 billion for 3,260 MWe of generating capacity, Hinkley Point C costs approximately £14,700 per kW in current prices — a figure that places it, alongside Vogtle, at the absolute top of the global nuclear cost distribution. Of the 39 nuclear plants constructed since 2000 with reliable cost data, Hinkley Point C ranks second from last. Only America's Vogtle 3 and 4 cost more per megawatt of generating capacity (Dumitriu, Infrastructure Costs: Nuclear Edition, 2023).

The project is financed by EDF — the French state-controlled utility — and China General Nuclear Power Group. EDF, already carrying debt of €54.5 billion at the time of re-nationalisation by the French government, is being asked to finance not only Hinkley Point C but also a new fleet of six EPR2 reactors in France, and potentially a second British nuclear station at Sizewell C. The financial logic is questionable: EDF is borrowing money at significant cost to build a plant that will sell electricity at a guaranteed "strike price" negotiated in 2016. The cost overruns have been partly blamed on Brexit-related supply chain disruptions, the COVID-19 pandemic, and inflation — but as a detailed account from the Energy Mix (2024) notes, the overoptimistic miscalculations made by EDF mean the original cost estimates were simply never credible.

The pattern is unmistakable. EDF, like Areva before it and Westinghouse before both, committed to a project at a price that reflected political desire rather than engineering reality. The contractor-state model in France may differ from the private contractor model in the United States, but when the same EPR design is applied in Finland, France (Flamanville 3), and the United Kingdom, it produces the same result: massively delayed, massively over-budget construction driven by first-of-a-kind construction challenges, inadequate project governance, and multi-layered subcontracting.

## 5. The CWIP Mechanism: How Ratepayers Subsidise Failure

### 5.1 Construction Work in Progress as a Structural Distortion

A full account of why Western nuclear power has become so expensive must address not only the contractor model but also the financial mechanism that shields contractors and utilities from the consequences of failure: Construction Work in Progress (CWIP) accounting.

CWIP allows utilities to add the cost of power plants currently under construction — along with a guaranteed rate of return on that cost — to their rate base, meaning customers pay for plants before they generate a single kilowatt of electricity. The mechanism was originally introduced to help utilities finance long-lead infrastructure projects. In practice, it has produced inflated budgets through undisciplined spending and pushed risk from investors onto ratepayers (Manhattan Institute, 2025).

The fundamental distortion is a matter of incentive engineering. Under conventional project financing, a utility bears the full cost of construction delays and overruns: interest compounds, profits are deferred, and shareholders absorb the penalty of poor execution. Under CWIP, every additional dollar spent and every month of delay expands the utility's rate base and increases its guaranteed returns (Utility Dive, 2025). The explicit framing of one academic study is worth quoting directly: cost overruns become profit opportunities rather than financial penalties.

This is not a minor structural quirk. It is a near-complete inversion of normal business risk. A construction management firm that delivers on time and on budget earns its contracted fee. A construction management firm operating within a CWIP-enabled rate-regulated utility earns more the longer it takes and the more it spends. The surprise is not that nuclear projects run over budget and schedule in this environment — the surprise is that anyone expected them not to.

### 5.2 Ratepayers Paying for Plants That Never Operated

The consequences of CWIP for the public were made vivid at V.C. Summer. Ratepayers in South Carolina had been paying increased utility bills to fund construction under the state's Base Load Review Act — a CWIP-enabling statute — from the beginning of the project. When the project was abandoned in 2017 with more than $10 billion spent, customers found themselves holding the bill for approximately $2 billion in charges for reactors that never generated electricity, and with no clear legal remedy (Choose Energy, 2020).

The V.C. Summer case was not unique. Across the American nuclear build-out of the 1970s and 1980s, ratepayers in Missouri and New Hampshire paid billions for reactors that were cancelled mid-construction. In Florida, Progress Energy received regulatory approval to charge ratepayers for construction work in progress on two new reactors at an expected cost of at least $17 billion — charges that raised customers' average utility bills by 10% — although the plants never came to fruition (UCS, 2019).

The CWIP mechanism also explains why utilities have limited incentive to push back aggressively on contractor costs. If the utility earns a regulated rate of return on every dollar in its rate base, then a billion-dollar cost overrun — which would be catastrophic for a normal business — actually increases its asset base, its rate base, and therefore its guaranteed long-run profits. The utility's shareholders do not lose from overruns under this regime; its ratepayers do. Reuters analysis found that electric utilities earn a regulated rate of return on capital spending that ranges from 9% to 12% (Reuters, 2026). In an environment of financial repression and low interest rates, a guaranteed 10% return on capital — regardless of whether the capital was efficiently deployed — is an attractive financial proposition. Extending the construction timeline, allowing contractor margins to expand, and tolerating cost overruns all increase the asset base on which that return is earned.

## 6. The International Comparison: Proof That Cheap Nuclear Is Still Possible

### 6.1 South Korea: $2,200/kW and On Schedule

The South Korean nuclear industry constitutes the single most powerful empirical refutation of the proposition that modern nuclear power is inherently expensive. South Korea has, in recent decades, consistently built reactors at overnight costs of approximately $2,200–$2,300 per kilowatt — roughly one-eighth the cost of Vogtle and less than one-sixth the cost of Hinkley Point C (IEA/NEA, *Projected Costs of Generating Electricity*, 2020; Latitude Media, 2025).

South Korea's domestic APR1400 units and the four-reactor Barakah project in the United Arab Emirates have generally experienced smaller cost overruns and schedule delays than recent Western projects like Vogtle (Schlanj, 2026). The Barakah project — four APR1400 reactors built in the UAE, a country with no prior nuclear history — was completed at a turnkey price of approximately $3,800/kW (Schlanj, 2026), which is still less than half the Vogtle cost and comparable to what American nuclear cost during its most efficient era.

The structural reason for South Korea's cost advantage is not a secret. South Korea doesn't have the project fragmentation problem. Private contractors were able to invest in skills and equipment over the long term secure in the knowledge that the government is committed to building multiple reactors. Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO), South Korea's national nuclear champion, retains design authority, construction management capability, and a continuous workforce across successive projects — the institutional continuity that Rickover's Naval Reactors program had in the United States, but which was never replicated in the American commercial sector (Dumitriu, 2023).

The contrast with the United States is stark. Westinghouse offered six AP1000 reactors to Poland for $31.3 billion, or $4,700/kW — and this was a post-Vogtle "lessons learned" price. KEPCO offered six APR1400 reactors for $26.7 billion, or $3,200/kW (Devanney, 2023). Westinghouse, unable to compete on price, pursued Westinghouse-owned intellectual property claims against KEPCO in court rather than improving its cost structure.

### 6.2 China: Volume, State Control, and the Learning Curve Working as It Should

China's nuclear industry offers a different but equally instructive counterexample. The IEA/NEA data places Chinese overnight nuclear construction costs at approximately $2,500/kW. China's indigenous Hualong One design has an announced upgraded version — the Hualong Two — with construction costs expected to fall to approximately $2,000/kW from the Hualong One's $2,600, and with construction time reducing from five years to four (World Nuclear Association, 2026).

China is achieving the cost trajectory that economic theory predicts for a scaling industry: learning-curve cost reduction driven by volume production and institutional continuity. China approves nuclear projects in batches — eleven reactors at five sites were approved in a single announcement in August 2024 — and the state-owned entities that build them (CNNC, CGN, and State Power Investment) carry institutional knowledge, workforce continuity, and supply chain relationships across projects in the way that KEPCO does in South Korea.

The IEA/NEA *Projected Costs of Generating Electricity* (2020) documents the result: overnight nuclear costs in South Korea and China are $2,157/kW and $2,500/kW respectively, while the US figure is not separately stated because Vogtle was the only plant under construction and its cost was so anomalous as to be unrepresentative. For context, the US EIA's 2024 estimate for new nuclear capacity in the United States is $7,821/kW — approximately three and a half times the South Korean figure, for the same generation of reactor technology.

### 6.3 The Identical Technology, the Radically Different Cost

Perhaps the most revealing single data point in the entire nuclear cost debate is this: the AP1000 reactor design — the same Westinghouse design that cost $17,500/kW per unit at Vogtle — has been built in China at costs of approximately $1,700–$2,500/kW (SCGI, 2023). The reactor design is the same. The physics is the same. The construction techniques are broadly comparable. The difference is entirely in how the project is governed, contracted, and managed.

In China, the AP1000 is built by SNPTC — a state-owned enterprise — with an integrated design-build capability and a continuous construction program. There are no layers of subcontractors with conflicting incentives. There is no CWIP mechanism enabling cost passthrough to captive ratepayers. There is no fragmented supply chain attempting to manufacture modular components without accumulated experience. The state organisation builds the reactor, carries the knowledge from project to project, and is accountable for the outcome.

The same reactor. One-seventh the cost. The variable is not the technology.

## 7. The Regulatory Question: A Real Factor, But Not the Primary One

It would be intellectually dishonest to ignore the role of regulation entirely. NRC regulatory requirements are genuinely more extensive than those of Korean or Chinese regulators, and the cumulative cost of compliance is substantial. The average nuclear plant bears an annual regulatory burden of approximately $60 million — including $8.6 million in regulatory costs, $22 million in fees to the NRC, and $32.7 million in regulatory liabilities (AAF, 2017). With 61 plants in commercial operation, this represents approximately $3.6 billion in annual ongoing regulatory burden for the industry (AAF, 2017).

The regulatory guide proliferation documented above — from 21 guides in 1971 to 143 in 1978 — imposed real material and labour cost increases. A 1980 study attributed a 176% increase in plant cost to increased regulation between the late 1960s and mid-1970s (IFP, 2025).

However, four considerations limit how much weight the regulatory explanation can carry.

First, regulation accounts for only a minority of the total cost increase. The MIT Eash-Gates study attributes only 30% of the 1976–1988 cost escalation to increased regulation; 70% was due to indirect labour costs — the contractor management stack (Eash-Gates et al., 2020).

Second, the US Navy operates under the same ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) safety philosophy adopted worldwide, and the same broad regulatory framework as commercial plants, yet has constructed and operated reactors for decades at costs that do not approach commercial nuclear. The regulatory burden falls equally on Navy and commercial nuclear; the cost difference is organisational.

Third, South Korea's nuclear regulator is not materially less rigorous than the NRC, yet Korean plants cost one-eighth as much. If regulation were the primary driver of cost, Korean and Chinese plants would face similar cost penalties. They do not.

Fourth, the NRC's own overhead is itself a symptom of the broader problem. The NRC's peer agencies spend about 20 to 32 percent on overhead costs, compared to 37 percent at NRC (AAF, 2017). The regulatory bureaucracy has grown to match the complexity of the contractor-driven procurement model it oversees, rather than the complexity of the underlying technology.

---

## 8. The Structural Argument: A Summary

The evidence assembled in this paper supports a clear structural diagnosis of the Western nuclear cost crisis:

**1. The contractor-subcontractor model eliminates accountability and institutional continuity.** When a utility contracts a vendor that subcontracts a construction manager that subcontracts specialist firms, no single organisation is fully accountable for project outcomes. Knowledge does not transfer between projects. Labour productivity collapses to one-thirteenth of expected levels.

**2. First-of-a-kind (FOAK) costs are guaranteed by a broken supply chain.** The thirty-year hiatus in US nuclear construction between the 1970s and the 2009 AP1000 projects meant that neither the contractor ecosystem nor the manufacturing supply chain had the accumulated experience to deliver a "proven" design. Every plant becomes effectively FOAK because the institutional knowledge from the last plant has dispersed.

**3. CWIP accounting makes delays profitable for utilities and utilities have limited incentive to discipline contractors.** When overruns increase the rate base and therefore guaranteed returns, utilities face no shareholder pressure to manage costs aggressively. The losses fall entirely on ratepayers, who have no market exit option.

**4. International comparisons prove that the problem is organisational, not physical.** The same reactor designs, the same fundamental engineering, and the same safety objectives are being met at one-seventh to one-eighth the per-kilowatt cost in state-managed, vertically integrated programs. The variable is not the physics.

**5. The criminal record at V.C. Summer shows that the incentive distortions are severe enough to produce fraud.** When the consequences of disclosing a project's true state are worse for an executive than committing federal fraud, the incentive structure has gone beyond dysfunction into systemic failure.

---

## 9. The Path Forward: What Government-Managed Nuclear Showed Was Possible

The argument of this paper is not that nuclear power is inherently unworkable or that commercial involvement in nuclear energy is always problematic. It is that the specific model of fragmented contractor-subcontractor procurement, CWIP-enabled cost passthrough, and institutional discontinuity that characterises Western nuclear construction has made nuclear unaffordable in the West, not because of the technology, but because of the governance.

The historical record demonstrates a clear alternative. Under Rickover's Naval model — direct government technical authority, integrated management, personal accountability, and continuous institutional learning — nuclear technology was built at costs and timescales that would make the Vogtle experience almost unrecognisable. Under South Korea's KEPCO model — a state-backed vertically integrated champion with continuous construction programs and retained institutional knowledge — the same generation of reactor technology costs $2,200/kW today.

The implication for policymakers considering nuclear expansion is stark. The question is not whether to build nuclear plants. The question is whether to build them through the model that consistently produces results costing $15,000–$17,000/kW, decades of delays, criminal prosecutions, and ratepayer losses — or the model that consistently produces results costing $2,200–$3,800/kW, completed broadly on schedule.

The United States and the United Kingdom have the most extensive nuclear heritage in the world. They developed the technology, demonstrated its viability, and trained generations of nuclear engineers and operators. That heritage is being squandered by an institutional model that treats the construction of nuclear power plants as a financial opportunity to be managed through contractor layers and cost-passthrough mechanisms rather than as an engineering challenge to be solved through direct technical accountability.

The Navy still builds nuclear reactors at world-standard cost and safety. The question is why that institutional model has never been applied to civilian nuclear power — and whether the answer to that question tells us more about the economics of energy policy or the economics of political power.

---

## 10. Conclusion

Modern Western nuclear power is not expensive because of what it is. It is expensive because of how it is built. The contractor-subcontractor model, amplified by CWIP accounting and insulated from competitive discipline by regulated monopoly utilities, has systematically inflated the cost of nuclear construction to levels that bear no relationship to the actual engineering challenge or to the costs achieved by state-managed programs using comparable technology.

The evidence is unambiguous. The United States government built more than fifty nuclear reactors in the 1950s and 1960s under direct management at costs that, in inflation-adjusted terms, are a fraction of modern commercial nuclear costs. Admiral Rickover's Naval Reactors program has operated more reactors than the entire US commercial fleet, for decades, with an exemplary safety record, at costs that have not exhibited the catastrophic escalation seen in the commercial sector. South Korea builds the same generation of reactor at $2,200/kW. China builds it at $2,500/kW. The United States built it at $17,500/kW. The difference is entirely attributable to organisational structure, institutional continuity, and financial incentive design.

The $35 billion Vogtle project, the $10 billion V.C. Summer abandonment, the €11 billion Olkiluoto 3 disaster, and the £48 billion Hinkley Point C are not outliers or bad luck. They are the predictable outputs of a system designed — whether intentionally or through regulatory and financial drift — to reward cost escalation rather than cost discipline. In that system, ratepayers and taxpayers are the ultimate guarantors of every decision made by contractors, utilities, and their executives, while the financial beneficiaries of those decisions are insulated from consequences.

Nuclear power could be cheap again. The historical record, and the contemporary evidence from Asia, proves it. What is required is not a new reactor design, a new fuel cycle, or a new safety philosophy. What is required is a new — or rather, an old — institutional model: direct government technical authority, integrated project management, continuous institutional learning, and financial accountability that falls on the organisations making decisions rather than on the populations who will one day consume the electricity, if the plant is ever finished.
