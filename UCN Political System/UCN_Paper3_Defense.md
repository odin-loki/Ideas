# Neutral but capable: UCN defense, security, technology and energy policy

**United Commonwealth Nations · UCN Research Series — Paper III**

*UCN Policy Research Institute*

Version 1.0 | 2025

## Abstract

This paper examines the defense, security, technology, and energy policies of the United Commonwealth Nations (UCN) doctrine. UCN adopts a posture described as 'neutral but capable' — maintaining non-aggression pacts and strategic autonomy while developing sovereign defense, energy, and technological capabilities that deter aggression and prevent external dependence. Key policies include a mixed nuclear program integrating power generation and a minimal deterrent capability; a super soldier reserve corps and universal national service; a technology sovereignty strategy based on open-source research and strategic knowledge acquisition; and a comprehensive data sovereignty framework. The paper situates these positions within contemporary literature on strategic autonomy, nuclear deterrence, and technology governance.

  


## 1. Introduction: The Strategic Rationale for Sovereign Capability

Australia's strategic position is shaped by its geography, its resource wealth, and its historical integration into Anglo-American security alliances. While these alliances have provided security since the Second World War, they have also generated dependency: dependency on foreign military hardware, foreign intelligence systems, foreign technology infrastructure, and foreign strategic decision-making. UCN treats this dependency as a sovereign risk.

UCN's defense and security framework is designed to reduce this dependency without provoking the adversarial relationships that a more aggressive posture would generate. The guiding principle — 'neutral but capable' — means that Australia maintains non-aggression pacts with as many nations as possible, participates in multilateral security arrangements selectively, but ensures that its sovereign defense capabilities are sufficient to deter aggression and respond effectively if deterrence fails.

## 2. Defense Posture: Non-Aggression and Strategic Alliances

### 2.1 The Non-Aggression Framework

UCN's foundational foreign policy instrument is the Non-Aggression Pact (NAP) — a bilateral agreement committing both parties to refrain from initiating military action against each other, to maintain diplomatic communication channels, and to resolve disputes through defined multilateral mechanisms. UCN seeks NAPs with as many nations as possible across the political spectrum, including nations that are not formal allies.

This is not naive pacifism. UCN retains full military capability, including a nuclear deterrent. The NAP framework is designed to manage the risk of inadvertent escalation, to preserve Australian diplomatic neutrality, and to create the maximum number of relationships that generate intelligence, trade, and security benefit.

### 2.2 Strategic Alliances

UCN maintains a small number of deep strategic alliances — defined as relationships involving military cooperation, joint research, joint operations, and intelligence sharing. These alliances are evaluated instrumentally: what capability do they add to Australia's sovereign defense posture, and what do they cost in terms of strategic autonomy?

Allies provide opportunities for joint space capability development, training exercises, weapons testing, and international sales of Australian defense materiel. The selection of primary allies is subject to the UCN evidence standard: the research literature on military alliance effectiveness and the geopolitical analysis of stable alliance partners in the Asia-Pacific context informs these choices.

### 2.3 Defense Manufacturing Sovereignty

UCN mandates that Australia develops and maintains domestic manufacturing capability across all major defense materiel categories: small arms and ammunition, armoured vehicles, naval vessels, aircraft, missiles, and electronic warfare systems. This does not preclude importing where domestic production is not viable. It does require that viable import substitution is actively pursued and that at least one domestic entity retains capability in each category.

Missile systems represent a particular priority. UCN proposes purchasing technical data packages from multiple international sources — the United States, Russia, and others — and using this acquired knowledge to design Australian-developed missile systems with performance parameters appropriate to the region. This 'buy the data, build the product' approach is consistent with the broader UCN philosophy of knowledge acquisition as the foundation for sovereign capability development.

## 3. The Nuclear Program: Power and Deterrence

### 3.1 Nuclear Energy Policy

Australia is uniquely positioned to develop a sovereign nuclear energy capability. The country holds approximately 28% of the world's known uranium reserves — among the largest national endowments globally — yet generates none of its electricity through nuclear power. UCN treats this as one of the most significant resource underutilisation failures in Australian economic history.

UCN's nuclear energy policy mandates the development of a complete civilian nuclear fuel cycle: uranium mining and processing, fuel fabrication, reactor operation, and waste management. The energy generation backbone is a fleet of nuclear power stations, built on the sites of retiring coal-fired generators, providing baseload zero-carbon electricity that complements a renewables grid.

On the question of reactor technology, UCN is agnostic between large conventional reactors and Small Modular Reactors (SMRs). The Australian Academy of Technological Sciences and Engineering's 2024 report notes that SMRs 'could potentially form part of Australia's future low-carbon energy mix, utilising existing transmission infrastructure and contributing to baseload power' but that 'as an emerging technology, in 2024 the cost and operational performance of this technology has not yet been demonstrated' (ATSE, 2024). UCN's evidence-based approach accepts this uncertainty and proposes a parallel strategy: proceeding with proven large-scale reactor technology in the near term while monitoring SMR commercialisation internationally.

The CSIRO GenCost 2024-25 analysis notes that nuclear is not currently the lowest-cost decarbonisation pathway — but UCN's nuclear program is not solely an energy cost optimization exercise. It is a strategic capability investment that provides energy sovereignty, fuel cycle expertise, and — critically — the foundational competencies required for nuclear deterrence.

### 3.2 Nuclear Deterrence

UCN proposes a minimal nuclear deterrent capability: up to ten nuclear weapons, held as a second-strike deterrent only. The sole purpose of this capability is to ensure that any nuclear strike against Australia results in a nuclear response against the attacking nation. UCN explicitly rejects first-use doctrine.

This minimal deterrent posture is consistent with deterrence theory's core insight: that the threat of unacceptable retaliation is sufficient to prevent nuclear aggression, and that large arsenals are neither necessary nor strategically stabilising. The UK's Trident program, with a comparable minimal deterrent rationale, provides a Westminster-tradition precedent.

UCN's nuclear deterrent posture is publicly declared — maximum transparency about capability and doctrine. This is consistent with UCN's broader open-source philosophy: capability is the deterrent, and concealment is unnecessary and potentially destabilising.

### 3.3 Ceramic-Coated Nuclear Fuels

UCN specifically adopts ceramic-coated nuclear fuel technology — TRISO (Tristructural Isotropic) fuel particles — as the preferred fuel form for its reactor program. TRISO fuel's ceramic coating provides inherent accident containment properties that prevent the kind of fuel damage that leads to the release of radioactive material in accident scenarios. This design choice reflects UCN's evidence-based approach to risk management: adopting proven technical solutions that eliminate categories of failure risk rather than managing those risks through procedural controls.

## 4. Military Human Capital: National Service and the Super Soldier Program

### 4.1 Universal National Service

UCN mandates one year of universal national service for all citizens at age 18. This program is emphatically not conventional military conscription. Service members do not handle weapons. The program focuses exclusively on fitness, practical life skills, emergency response capability, systems thinking, and teamwork. Training is substantially simulation-based.

The rationale is multidimensional. National service builds physical fitness, civic engagement, cross-cultural social cohesion, and a universal baseline of emergency response capability across the population. It ensures that every Australian adult has been trained in basic first aid, disaster response, and community resilience skills — providing a formidable civilian emergency response reserve. It also creates a culture of shared civic obligation that research in social cohesion suggests is beneficial for long-term societal trust.

### 4.2 The Super Soldier Program

UCN establishes a select Super Soldier program — an elite military formation trained to special forces standards, augmented by research-based optimisation across physical performance, cognitive enhancement, and medical capability. Super Soldiers complete an intensive active service period and are then released to a flexible reserve commitment, maintaining civilian careers while remaining available for recall.

Research outputs from the Super Soldier program — in nutrition, cognitive performance, physical training methodology, pharmacological enhancement, and medical trauma management — are made fully public. This is consistent with UCN's open-source philosophy: defense investment in human performance research generates public health benefits that UCN maximises by mandating open publication. The capability itself — the trained individuals, their institutional knowledge, and their unit cohesion — remains sovereign. The underlying research is public.

Bioethical constraints on the Super Soldier program are set at the UN threshold: what is permissible under international humanitarian law and the relevant UN conventions on human subjects research. Within this constraint, UCN adopts a liberal interpretation, accepting that significant performance enhancement is both ethically permissible and strategically valuable.

## 5. Technology Sovereignty

### 5.1 The Take-Fork-Build Model

UCN's approach to technology sovereignty follows a three-phase model: Take — acquire all available foreign technology and knowledge through legitimate purchase, open-source access, and legal reverse engineering. Fork — create Australian variants of critical technologies, customised for sovereign control and local conditions. Build — gradually develop fully sovereign Australian alternatives, reducing dependency as capability matures.

In the initial phase, investment in foreign technology purchases is high. UCN explicitly prioritises buying technical data over buying hardware: a data package that enables domestic production of a weapons system, a drug compound, or an electronic component is more valuable than imported units of the end product.

This 'buy the knowledge, build the product' philosophy is applied across defense materiel, industrial equipment, pharmaceutical compounds, agricultural technology, and digital infrastructure.

### 5.2 Semiconductor and Electronics Manufacturing

UCN identifies semiconductor manufacturing capability as a critical strategic gap. Australia currently has no domestic semiconductor wafer fabrication capability, making it entirely dependent on imports for the electronic components underpinning its defense systems, critical infrastructure, and digital economy.

UCN mandates the development of at least one domestic semiconductor manufacturing facility — beginning with mature process nodes (where the technology is well-understood and tooling costs are substantially lower than for cutting-edge nodes) and progressing toward more advanced capability as the industrial base matures. Technical data acquisition from existing manufacturers — both through commercial licensing and through research partnerships with universities and national laboratories — underpins this development.

### 5.3 Operating System and Software Sovereignty

UCN mandates that all government systems and critical infrastructure operate on Australian-forked operating systems and software stacks, maintained by sovereign teams. This addresses the risk that foreign technology vendors — through deliberate backdoors, accidental vulnerabilities, or supply chain attacks — could compromise Australian government or infrastructure systems.

Commercial foreign software continues to be used where the security risk is manageable and no sovereign alternative yet exists. But the trajectory is explicit: over time, every critical government and infrastructure system migrates to sovereign software. Australia's existing quantum computing research — centred at the University of Sydney and related institutions — provides a foundation for next-generation sovereign computing capability.

### 5.4 Intellectual Property: Citizens Only

UCN introduces a radical reform to intellectual property law: only Australian citizens may hold IP rights recognised under Australian law. Foreign-held IP may be used freely within Australia — treated as a knowledge commons available to citizens and government. This inverts the conventional IP regime, which treats foreign IP holders as legally equivalent to domestic holders.

This policy reflects UCN's philosophy that knowledge generated outside Australia's borders belongs to the global commons and cannot legitimately constrain Australian sovereign capability development. It also provides a powerful incentive for talented individuals to obtain Australian citizenship — since citizenship confers IP protection unavailable to non-citizens.

## 6. Data Sovereignty and Privacy

### 6.1 Government as Sole Data Collector

UCN establishes that the Australian government is the sole entity authorised to collect, aggregate, and sell population-level data. Corporations operating in Australia may not independently collect and monetise personal data about Australian citizens. The government collects data — through the full range of legitimate government functions — anonymises it rigorously, and sells anonymised data products to commercial entities that require population-level insights.

This framework eliminates the current model in which tech giants like Google, Meta, and data brokers collect and commercialise Australian citizens' personal data for the benefit of foreign shareholders. Under UCN, this data asset belongs to Australia — and the revenue from its commercialisation accrues to the public exchequer.

### 6.2 Citizen Rights

Citizens retain the right to access all data held about them by the government, and receive a categorical guarantee that individually-identifiable data will never be sold or shared with commercial entities. Intelligence sharing with foreign governments occurs only by court order and under formal inter-governmental agreement.

Social media companies may purchase anonymised demographic data from the government data authority, but may not conduct independent user tracking or profiling. This eliminates the surveillance capitalism model in Australia without eliminating the utility of digital platforms for citizens.

## 7. Space and Satellite Capability

UCN treats space capability as a shared strategic asset, developed in partnership with a primary ally and selectively opened to non-aggression pact partners. A sovereign launch program — initially modest in capability, targeting small satellite deployment for communications and earth observation — is developed using existing Australian aerospace engineering talent augmented by strategic knowledge acquisition from established space powers.

Navigation capability (a sovereign GPS equivalent) is developed jointly with the primary ally and made available for both military and civilian use. Military satellites are treated as a shared capability with the primary ally; civilian communications and earth observation satellites are operated as public infrastructure.

## 8. Energy Infrastructure: Modular and Government-Owned

UCN mandates a complete redesign and government takeover of Australia's electricity grid. The current grid — a legacy patchwork of state-owned and privatised assets with inadequate interconnection and poor maintainability — is replaced with a purpose-designed modular architecture: nuclear baseload, renewable variable generation, and grid-scale storage, interconnected through a modernised national transmission system.

The grid is government-owned and government-operated. Private generation is permitted and incentivised, but transmission infrastructure is a public asset. This ensures that network investment serves national energy security objectives rather than private investor returns.

UCN's vision for transport energy is complementary: development of nuclear battery technology (radioisotope thermal generators and related technologies at vehicular scale) to enable green road transport without dependence on the international lithium battery supply chain. A government-funded automotive research enterprise develops vehicle platforms around this technology.

## 9. Conclusion

UCN's defense, security, technology, and energy framework is united by a single strategic imperative: sovereign capability. Australia must be capable of defending itself, powering itself, computing for itself, and manufacturing for itself — not because international engagement is undesirable, but because engagement without capability is dependency.

The 'neutral but capable' posture positions Australia as a strategic actor that cannot be ignored, coerced, or dependent — while maintaining the flexibility to engage constructively with the full range of international actors through non-aggression pacts, strategic alliances, and open knowledge-sharing protocols.
