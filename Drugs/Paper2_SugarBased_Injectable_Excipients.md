# Sugar-based excipient systems for injectable pharmaceutical formulations

**Design principles, long-shelf-life optimisation, and reconstitution for parenteral administration**

*Pharmaceutical Sciences — Formulation Chemistry & Parenteral Drug Delivery*

## Abstract

Sugar-based excipients constitute the most broadly utilised class of inactive pharmaceutical ingredients in both solid and injectable dosage forms. Mannitol, trehalose, sucrose, and lactose each offer distinct physicochemical profiles — solubility, compressibility, hygroscopicity, reducing sugar chemistry, and glass transition temperature — that govern their suitability for specific routes of administration and storage conditions \[1\]\[2\]. Of these, mannitol holds a uniquely privileged position in injectable pharmaceutical science: it is FDA-approved for intravenous use at clinical doses \(Osmitrol\), is the dominant lyophilisation excipient in biopharmaceutical manufacture, and forms free-flowing crystalline cakes suitable for reconstitution \[3\]\[13\]. This paper provides a comprehensive review of sugar-based excipient science with specific focus on the design of long-shelf-life, reconstitutable injectable formulations. We examine the physicochemical basis of excipient selection, formulation composition principles following the Universal Mannitol Formulation \(UMF\) framework, lyophilisation cycle design, quality control methodology against USP-NF and European Pharmacopoeia standards \[18\]\[24\], stability testing to ICH guidelines, and the critical particulate, sterility, and endotoxin requirements governing parenteral acceptance criteria. The paper is intended as a practical academic reference for pharmaceutical scientists engaged in injectable formulation development.

**Keywords:** *mannitol, pharmaceutical excipients, lyophilisation, injectable formulation, parenteral drug delivery, sugar-based matrix, freeze-drying, reconstitution, long-shelf-life, USP-NF*

## 1. Introduction

Pharmaceutical excipients — the inactive ingredients of drug products — perform functions that are anything but passive. In injectable formulations, they govern solubility, stability, tonicity, pH, viscosity, reconstitution behaviour, and long-term shelf life under conditions of temperature, humidity, and light stress \[1\]\[4\]. Sugar-based excipients have been central to pharmaceutical formulation science since the earliest days of industrial drug manufacturing, and they remain indispensable in modern biopharmaceutical development, where they serve as the primary stabilisers for protein therapeutics, vaccines, and complex biologics prepared by lyophilisation \[3\]\[6\].

The selection of a sugar excipient for an injectable formulation is not trivial. Reducing sugars \(glucose, lactose\) undergo Maillard reaction with primary amines — including lysine residues in protein active pharmaceutical ingredients — leading to covalent adduct formation, loss of potency, and generation of reactive degradation products \[9\]\[10\]. Sucrose, while non-reducing, is hygroscopic and prone to crystallisation-induced phase separation during freeze-drying \[7\]. Trehalose offers exceptional thermodynamic stabilisation of protein structure in the dry state but is considerably more expensive and less widely available than mannitol \[6\]\[29\]. Mannitol, a sugar alcohol \(polyol\) derived by reduction of mannose, combines favourable chemical inertness, crystallinity, low hygroscopicity, and regulatory precedent for injectable use to emerge as the universal excipient of choice for reconstitutable parenteral formulations \[13\]\[24\].

This paper reviews the scientific basis for sugar excipient selection in injectable formulations, describes the Universal Mannitol Formulation \(UMF\) framework and its component rationale, provides detailed analysis of lyophilisation cycle design, and addresses the quality, regulatory, and stability standards applicable to products of this class. The goal is to provide a scientifically rigorous and practically useful reference for formulation scientists working in this space.

### 2.2 Mannitol: Detailed Profile

Mannitol \(D-mannitol, C₆H₁₄O₆, MW 182.17 g/mol\) is a hexitol occurring naturally in various plants and fungi, and produced industrially by catalytic hydrogenation of mannose/fructose mixtures \[13\]\[24\]. Its key physicochemical attributes for pharmaceutical use are:

- Chemical inertness: No aldehyde or keto group; no Maillard reactivity with protein APIs $\{r9\}
- Low hygroscopicity: Equilibrium moisture content <0.5% at 75% RH, enabling long-term storage stability without sealed foil overwrap $\{r1\}
- Crystallinity: Preferential crystallisation during lyophilisation produces a rigid, mechanically stable cake resistant to collapse and providing excellent reconstitution characteristics $\{r3\}$\{r25\}
- Isotonicity contribution: A 5.07% w/v mannitol solution is isotonic with blood plasma \(285–295 mOsm/kg\), simplifying tonicity adjustment $\{r4\}
- Multiple polymorphic forms: α, β, δ, and ε mannitol — the β form is most thermodynamically stable and preferred in pharmaceutical manufacture $\{r13\}
- Cryoprotection: Mannitol provides mechanical support as a bulking agent in lyophilised formulations but has limited amorphous-phase protein stabilisation relative to trehalose or sucrose $\{r6\}$\{r29\}

Mannitol's regulatory dossier is exceptionally comprehensive. USP-NF monograph 'Mannitol for Injection' \(Osmitrol\) establishes compendial identity, purity, and limit tests \[18\]. The European Pharmacopoeia monograph 0559 specifies HPLC purity criteria, heavy metals limits, and specific rotation tolerances \[24\]. These standards collectively define pharmaceutical-grade mannitol suitable for injectable use.

### 2.3 Trehalose

Trehalose \(α,α-trehalose, MW 342.30 g/mol\) is a non-reducing disaccharide with exceptional protein-stabilising properties in the dry state, attributable to preferential exclusion from protein surfaces in solution and direct water-replacement \(vitrification\) of protein surface hydrogen bonds during desiccation \[7\]\[29\]. Its Tg in the amorphous state \(approximately 115°C\) substantially exceeds that of sucrose \(~75°C\), providing superior protection against collapse and crystallisation during lyophilisation and storage \[8\]. Trehalose is the preferred excipient in formulations of highly labile monoclonal antibodies and vaccines where long-term solid-state protein structural integrity is paramount \[10\]. Its higher cost and limited regulatory dossier for IV dosing relative to mannitol have restricted broader adoption in commodity injectable formulations \[6\].

### 2.4 Sucrose

Sucrose \(MW 342.30 g/mol\) is the most widely used sugar excipient in lyophilised biopharmaceutical formulations globally, offering potent amorphous-phase protein stabilisation through hydrogen-bond substitution and vitrification \[3\]. However, sucrose is hygroscopic \(critical relative humidity ~75%\) and subject to crystallisation-induced phase separation during slow freezing, which can lead to protein exclusion from the stabilising amorphous matrix \[33\]. Sucrose is also susceptible to acid hydrolysis to glucose and fructose at low pH, and the resulting reducing sugars can then cause Maillard degradation. Despite these caveats, sucrose remains the dominant excipient in marketed biopharmaceutical lyophilisates due to its protein stabilisation efficacy and low cost \[3\].

### 2.5 Why Mannitol Dominates Injectable Formulation Design

The convergence of regulatory approval for IV administration, crystalline cake-forming behaviour during lyophilisation, chemical inertness toward protein APIs, low hygroscopicity, and cost-effectiveness makes mannitol the singular recommended excipient for reconstitutable parenteral formulations intended for clinical use. The Universal Mannitol Formulation framework described in the following section codifies this accumulated pharmaceutical science into a practical starting-point composition \[1\]\[13\]\[24\].

### 3.2 Component Functions in Detail

### *3.2.1 Mannitol \(85–90%\)*

Mannitol serves as the primary bulk component providing structural integrity to the lyophilised cake. During freeze-drying, mannitol crystallises preferentially from the amorphous frozen matrix, yielding a rigid, white, porous crystalline cake with high surface area that reconstitutes rapidly upon addition of sterile water for injection \(WFI\) \[3\]\[25\]. The crystalline structure ensures the cake does not collapse during primary drying at temperatures above Tg', as would occur with amorphous excipients such as sucrose or trehalose at high loading \[8\]. At 85–90% of the matrix, mannitol also provides the primary contribution to reconstituted solution osmolality — 5.07% w/v mannitol produces approximately 280 mOsm/kg, close to physiological isotonicity \[4\].

### *3.2.2 Microcrystalline Cellulose \(5–10%\)*

Microcrystalline cellulose \(MCC, Avicel PH series\) functions as a binding agent within the solid matrix, maintaining powder cohesion during manufacturing steps including granulation, blending, and fill. At 5–10% w/w, MCC does not compromise reconstitution time because its particle size and porosity allow rapid water penetration \[14\]. MCC also acts as a flow aid, improving powder uniformity during fill operations — a critical quality attribute for dose accuracy in injectable unit-dose vials \[1\].

### *3.2.3 Croscarmellose Sodium \(2–3%\)*

Croscarmellose sodium \(Ac-Di-Sol\) is a cross-linked sodium carboxymethylcellulose superdisintegrant. Upon contact with water during reconstitution, croscarmellose rapidly absorbs water \(up to 10× its own weight\), swells, and disrupts the matrix structure, driving rapid, complete dissolution of the lyophilised cake \[1\]\[23\]. At 2–3% w/w, croscarmellose typically achieves reconstitution times of 30–60 seconds for well-formulated mannitol-based lyophilisates — substantially faster than the 2–5 minutes commonly seen with mannitol alone \[13\]. Compatibility with the API must be confirmed, as croscarmellose can bind cationic drugs and reduce bioavailability in oral formulations; for injectable reconstitution this interaction is less problematic given the short contact time.

### *3.2.4 Magnesium Stearate \(0.5–1%\)*

Magnesium stearate is a hydrophobic lubricant widely used in pharmaceutical manufacturing to prevent adhesion of powder to tooling and container surfaces during filling operations. At the low levels used \(0.5–1%\), its effect on reconstitution time is negligible, but it dramatically reduces processing waste and improves fill weight uniformity \[14\]. For injectable formulations, pharmaceutical-grade magnesium stearate with specified heavy metal limits must be used. There is a theoretical concern about lubricant-induced surface hydrophobicity of reconstituted particles — this should be evaluated during formulation development using dynamic light scattering.

### 3.3 Buffer and Tonicity Adjustment for Injectable Use

The UMF framework describes the dry excipient matrix. For injectable administration, reconstitution with sterile WFI produces a suspension or solution that must meet parenteral pH, tonicity, and particulate specifications \[18\]\[24\]. Standard additions to the reconstitution vehicle or incorporated into the lyophilised matrix include:

- Buffer system: Phosphate \(pH 6.5–7.5\), histidine \(pH 5.5–7.0\), or citrate \(pH 3.5–6.0\) at 10–20 mM — selected based on API pH stability window $\{r4\}
- Tonicity adjustment: Sodium chloride \(0.9% w/v isotonic\) or mannitol \(5% w/v isotonic\) added to reconstituted volume to achieve 280–320 mOsm/kg $\{r18\}
- Cryoprotectant/lyoprotectant: Trehalose or sucrose at 0.5–2% w/w may be added if the API requires amorphous-phase stabilisation during lyophilisation $\{r6\}$\{r7\}
- Surfactant: Polysorbate 20 or 80 at 0.01–0.1% w/v to prevent API aggregation during reconstitution and agitation $\{r10\}

## 4. Lyophilisation Cycle Design

### 4.1 Principles of Freeze-Drying

Lyophilisation \(freeze-drying\) is the process of removing water from a frozen product by sublimation under reduced pressure, yielding a dry solid that can be reconstituted rapidly and stored for years at ambient temperature \[25\]\[26\]. For injectable sugar-based formulations, lyophilisation is the preferred preservation method when API solutions are too unstable for liquid storage — as is the case for virtually all proteins, many peptides, and numerous chemically reactive small molecules \[3\]\[27\].

The lyophilisation cycle comprises three phases: \(1\) freezing, in which the product solution is cooled to the eutectic or glass transition temperature of the maximally freeze-concentrated solution \(Tg'\); \(2\) primary drying, in which the ice phase is removed by sublimation at pressure below the ice vapour pressure and shelf temperature above Tg' but below the product collapse temperature; and \(3\) secondary drying, in which residual adsorbed water is desorbed at elevated temperature to achieve final moisture content below 1–3% w/w \[8\]\[25\].

### 4.3 Mannitol-Specific Cycle Considerations

Mannitol crystallisation during freeze-drying is strongly influenced by cooling rate and annealing conditions \[33\]. At slow cooling rates \(0.5°C/min\), mannitol crystallises during freezing, yielding the β polymorph associated with optimal cake structure \[13\]. At rapid cooling rates, mannitol may remain amorphous in the frozen state — this is undesirable as amorphous mannitol has poor mechanical stability and higher hygroscopicity \[3\]. An annealing step \(hold at −20°C for 2–4 hours before resuming cooling\) promotes complete crystallisation and is standard practice in mannitol lyophilisation cycle design \[8\]\[25\]. Residual amorphous mannitol can be detected by X-ray powder diffraction \(XRPD\) or differential scanning calorimetry \(DSC\) and should be minimised to <5% of total mannitol content \[31\].

The collapse temperature of mannitol-containing formulations is typically in the range −28°C to −32°C \(Tg' values from Table 1\). Primary drying shelf temperature must remain below this value to prevent structural collapse — loss of cake integrity, reduced surface area, and extended reconstitution time \[8\]\[26\]. For formulations containing amorphous co-excipients \(sucrose, trehalose\) alongside mannitol, the effective collapse temperature is a weighted average of the two phases and must be determined experimentally by freeze-dry microscopy \[32\].

### 4.4 Moisture Content and Residual Water

Residual moisture in lyophilised products is a primary driver of solid-state degradation, including protein aggregation, Maillard browning, and small-molecule hydrolysis \[28\]\[29\]. Target residual moisture for mannitol-based injectable lyophilisates is typically <2% w/w by Karl Fischer titration, corresponding to a water activity \(aw\) below 0.3 at ambient temperature. At this moisture level, most chemical degradation reactions are kinetically retarded over typical 2–5-year shelf-life durations when stored at 2–8°C or even 25°C/60% RH in sealed vials \[3\]\[29\]. Container-closure integrity is critical: rubber-stoppered glass vials with aluminium crimp seals provide the primary moisture barrier, and stopper water vapour transmission rate must be characterised as part of the primary packaging qualification \[5\]\[30\].

## 5. Quality Control and Compendial Testing

### 5.1 Applicable Pharmacopoeial Standards

Parenteral dosage forms are subject to the most stringent quality standards in pharmaceutical manufacturing. Injectable mannitol-based formulations must comply with the following compendial requirements \[17\]\[18\]\[24\]:

- USP <1>: Injections and Implanted Drug Products — general principles of sterility, container requirements, and labelling
- USP <85>: Bacterial Endotoxins Test \(LAL method\) — limits typically 0.5 EU/mL for IV products or 5 EU/kg body weight per hour for large-volume parenterals
- USP <788>: Particulate Matter in Injections — ≤6000 particles ≥10 μm and ≤600 particles ≥25 μm per container
- EP 2.9.19 / USP <2>: Particulate contamination for visible particles — 100% visual inspection under controlled illumination
- USP <71>: Sterility tests — membrane filtration or direct inoculation into growth media per product classification
- EP 0559 / USP Mannitol monograph: Identification by HPLC and optical rotation; assay ≥98.0%–101.5% w/w; sulphated ash, heavy metals, reducing sugars limits

### 5.3 Stability Testing Programme

Stability testing follows ICH Q1A\(R2\) guidelines for new drug products \[17\]. Long-term conditions: 25°C/60% RH for 24 months \(primary shelf-life support\). Accelerated conditions: 40°C/75% RH for 6 months \(predictive of 25°C shelf life via Arrhenius relationships\). Intermediate conditions: 30°C/65% RH for 12 months \(where 25°C long-term data show out-of-trend results\).

For lyophilised injectables, stability-indicating methods must demonstrate that the assay is capable of detecting all known API degradation pathways. Key stability-indicating tests include HPLC with photodiode array detection for related substances, SEC-HPLC or DLS for protein aggregation monitoring, Karl Fischer titration for moisture trending, and visual inspection for cake appearance changes \[10\]\[31\]. Reconstitution performance should be assessed at each stability timepoint as a surrogate for physical integrity.

## 6. Reconstitution for Injectable Administration

### 6.1 Sterile Reconstitution Requirements

Reconstitution of a lyophilised injectable must be performed under aseptic conditions to preserve the sterility of the final preparation \[18\]. In clinical settings, this is performed in a laminar air flow \(LAF\) cabinet using sterile WFI or other pharmacopoeial-grade diluent specified in the product label. The volume of reconstitution diluent determines the final drug concentration; overfill of the lyophilised vial \(typically 10–15% excess fill\) compensates for wetting losses and ensures the labelled dose can be extracted \[1\].

### 6.2 Particulate Concerns in Reconstitution

Reconstitution-induced particulate formation is a significant quality risk for injectable preparations. Incomplete dissolution of excipients, API aggregation triggered by agitation, pH shift during reconstitution, or leachables from rubber stoppers can all contribute to visible or subvisible particulate loads \[4\]\[10\]. The UMF formulation's inclusion of croscarmellose sodium as a superdisintegrant is specifically designed to promote complete, rapid dissolution and minimise particulate residuals. Post-reconstitution visual inspection for turbidity, colour change, and visible particles is required before every administration \[18\]\[24\].

### 6.3 Storage After Reconstitution

Reconstituted solutions are chemically and microbiologically less stable than the lyophilised product. Typical hold times after reconstitution are 2–24 hours at 2–8°C, or up to 6 hours at room temperature, subject to API-specific stability data \[3\]\[16\]. Reconstituted mannitol formulations should not be frozen, as re-crystallisation of mannitol may cause volumetric expansion and particulate formation. Multi-dose vial formulations require preservatives \(typically benzyl alcohol 0.9%\) to maintain microbiological integrity after first puncture \[4\]$.

## 7. Long-Shelf-Life Design Principles

### 7.1 Factors Governing Shelf Life

The shelf life of lyophilised injectable formulations is determined by the rate of physicochemical degradation reactions in the solid state: chemical reactions \(oxidation, hydrolysis, Maillard browning, racemisation\), physical changes \(crystallisation, aggregation, polymorphic transitions\), and biological changes \(loss of potency, development of immunogenic aggregates for protein APIs\) \[28\]\[29\]. The Arrhenius equation governs temperature-dependence of these rates, and the experimentally determined activation energies inform the shelf-life prediction under real storage conditions from accelerated stability data \[35\].

For mannitol-based lyophilisates, the three key design parameters for maximising shelf life are: \(1\) residual moisture below 2% w/w — the single most important predictor of solid-state reaction rate \[28\]\[29\]; \(2\) container-closure integrity — glass vials with low water vapour transmission stoppers maintain headspace humidity below the product's critical RH throughout shelf life \[5\]\[30\]; and \(3\) storage temperature — every 10°C reduction in storage temperature approximately halves the rate of most solid-state degradation reactions \(Q10 factor ~2\) \[35\].

### 7.2 Target Shelf Life of 2–5 Years

A well-designed mannitol lyophilisate stored at 2–8°C can typically support a 2–5 year shelf life for most small-molecule APIs and a 2–3 year shelf life for protein APIs, provided residual moisture is ≤2%, container-closure integrity is qualified, and API-specific chemical degradation rates are low \[3\]\[6\]. The pharmaceutical literature documents approved products with shelf lives of 24–36 months under refrigerated conditions and up to 24 months at room temperature for particularly stable formulations \[16\]\[17\]. Achieving ≥36 months at room temperature \(25°C/60% RH\) is feasible for chemically stable small-molecule APIs in mannitol matrices with optimised primary drying endpoints and qualified container-closure systems \[8\]\[30\].

### 7.3 Cost-Effectiveness Analysis

Mannitol is commercially available from multiple qualified pharmaceutical suppliers \(Roquette, Merck KGaA, Cargill\) at pharmaceutical-grade purity at approximately USD 5–15/kg in bulk quantities, compared to trehalose at USD 50–150/kg and sucrose at USD 3–8/kg. The excipient material cost differential is largely offset by trehalose's superior stabilisation efficiency \(lower required loading\), but for commodity injectable formulations where both cost and regulatory simplicity are priorities, the mannitol-based UMF framework offers the most favourable overall development economics \[1\]\[13\].

## 8. Discussion

The Universal Mannitol Formulation represents the crystallisation of decades of pharmaceutical excipient science into a practical, cost-effective, and regulatory-precedented framework for injectable lyophilisate development. Its central components — mannitol as bulking/crystallising agent, MCC as binder, croscarmellose sodium as disintegrant, and magnesium stearate as lubricant — each serve clearly defined, non-redundant functions that have been individually and collectively characterised in the pharmaceutical literature \[1\]\[13\]\[14\].

The framework's primary limitation is its focus on excipient function without prescribing API compatibility testing — a step that must be conducted case by case. Reducing sugars incompatible with amine-containing APIs, oxidation-sensitive APIs incompatible with certain co-excipients, and moisture-sensitive APIs requiring ultra-low residual water all demand formulation-specific adaptations of the base composition \[9\]\[28\]. The framework is a starting point, not a universal solution.

The evolution of lyophilisation technology — spray freeze-drying, atmospheric freeze-drying, continuous lyophilisation — alongside advances in container-closure systems \(advanced coated stoppers, form-fill-seal systems, dual-chamber syringes\) continues to expand the design space available to injectable formulation scientists \[11\]\[33\]. The pharmacopoeial standards governing these products \(USP-NF, European Pharmacopoeia\) are regularly updated to reflect these developments, and formulation scientists should consult current editions rather than relying on historical limit values \[18\]\[24\].

Looking forward, the intersection of sugar-based excipient science with biopharmaceutical delivery — particularly long-acting injectable depots as described in companion work on GlycoDur-P and NutriComplete-P — represents a frontier where the principles outlined here \(lyoprotection, controlled solid-state crystallinity, injectable reconstitution requirements\) will need to be integrated with the challenges of high-mass macromolecular payloads and extended-duration depot stability.

## 9. Conclusions

Sugar-based excipient systems, and mannitol in particular, underpin the safe, effective, and shelf-stable delivery of injectable pharmaceutical products across the spectrum from small molecules to complex biologics. The Universal Mannitol Formulation framework provides a scientifically grounded, regulatory-precedented composition space for developing lyophilised injectables with 2–5 year shelf lives under appropriate storage conditions. Adherence to USP-NF and European Pharmacopoeia compendial standards for sterility, endotoxin content, particulate load, residual moisture, and API potency is essential and non-negotiable for any parenteral product entering clinical use. The combination of established excipient science, controlled lyophilisation cycle design, qualified container-closure systems, and ICH-aligned stability programmes provides a robust platform for the development of injectable formulations meeting the highest standards of pharmaceutical quality.
