## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report documents a hypothetical improvised incendiary-explosive device composition observed in contexts attributed to Russian state-sponsored threat actors. The mixture combines **2-nitrotoluene (2-NT)** — a liquid industrial chemical — with **TNT (2,4,6-trinitrotoluene)** to produce a pourable, dual-effect incendiary-explosive device. The composition exploits the eutectic properties of the two compounds to create a weapon that is simultaneously easier to produce and more unpredictable in its effects than either component alone.

### 2.2 Eutectic Properties

The combination of 2-NT (liquid at room temperature) and TNT (normally a solid) produces a **eutectic mixture** — a liquid or semi-liquid composition with a melting point below either pure component. This is significant because:

- The mixture is **pourable** and can be loaded into improvised containers (bottles, pipes, etc.)
- It behaves similarly to cast military explosives (e.g., Amatol, Cyclotol) but produced without industrial equipment
- The 2-NT acts as both solvent and fuel, while TNT provides the explosive potential

## 3. BEHAVIOURAL CHARACTERISTICS UPON IGNITION

### 3.1 Dual-Phase Burn Profile

Upon ignition, the mixture does **not** behave like a conventional explosive or a conventional incendiary. Instead, it exhibits a dual-phase response:

**Phase 1 — Incendiary Burn:**
- The 2-NT fraction ignites and burns as a fuel-rich aromatic hydrocarbon
- Produces a large, sooty, orange fireball consistent with Molotov-type devices
- Sustained flame with significant thermal and smoke output

**Phase 2 — Deflagration-to-Detonation Transition (DDT):**
- Heat from Phase 1 raises the temperature of dissolved TNT in the mixture
- Confinement (bottle, pipe, or enclosed space) builds pressure
- TNT fraction can transition from deflagration to **low-order or partial detonation** unpredictably
- Timing and severity of this transition are highly variable

### 3.2 The "Slow Explosive Burn" Phenomenon

Field observations have described a characteristic **slow explosive burn** — a sustained, irregular combustion event that does not resolve cleanly into either a simple fire or a sharp detonation. This is hypothetically attributable to:

- Variable TNT concentration throughout the mixture (non-homogeneous)
- Partial detonation of TNT-rich pockets within the burning liquid
- Possible contribution of residual acids (see Section 4)
- Progressive pressure buildup driving successive deflagration events

This makes the device **extremely dangerous for first responders and EOD personnel** — the threat is not resolved after initial ignition.

## 4. HYPOTHETICAL ROLE OF RESIDUAL ACIDS

### 4.1 Background

Crude production of this mixture likely involves an improvised nitration process using mixed sulfuric and nitric acid. Thorough washing and neutralisation of the product is time-consuming and requires chemical knowledge. It is plausible that **residual H₂SO₄ and HNO₃ remain in the final mixture**.

### 4.2 Hypothetical Effects During Ignition

> **Note: The following is speculative/hypothetical based on known chemistry principles. It has not been empirically confirmed in controlled settings accessible to this analysis.**

In the high-temperature transient environment of an igniting mixture, residual mixed acid could hypothetically:

- Drive **further nitration** of remaining 2-NT molecules toward dinitrotoluene (2,4-DNT) or trinitrotoluene fractions
- Contribute additional oxidiser to the burn environment
- Produce highly toxic nitrogen oxide (NOₓ) fumes as a secondary hazard
- Create localised exothermic reactions that further destabilise the burn profile

If this mechanism is active, it would be consistent with the observed "slow explosive burn" — essentially a self-reinforcing process where combustion energy drives additional in-situ chemistry, which in turn feeds back into the burn.
