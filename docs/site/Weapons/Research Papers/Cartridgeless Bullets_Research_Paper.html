Biopolymere Caseless (BPC) System

A Theoretical Framework for Protein-Based Ammunition Architecture with

Dual-Mode Ignition, Energetic Propellants, and a Pathway to Industrial Scale

Technical Research Paper — Defence Materials Science & Ammunition Engineering

2025

Abstract

This paper presents a comprehensive theoretical framework for a next-generation fully consumable small arms cartridge: the Biopolymere Caseless (BPC) system. The design eliminates metallic and polymer casings entirely, replacing them with a disulfide cross-linked recombinant protein matrix engineered from synthetic spider silk derivatives. The energetic charge is a nitrated poly-amino acid solid whose combustion chemistry mirrors nitrocellulose but derives from a biological backbone, achieving elevated volumetric energy density with significantly cleaner exhaust products. Thermal management is addressed through endothermic disulfide-bond cleavage and a high-ignition-temperature coating analogous to Dynamit Nobel's HITP formulation developed for the HK G11.

A dual-mode ignition system is proposed: a primary mechanical striker designed to interact directly with an exposed percussion-sensitive surface on the propellant block, with an optional secondary electrothermal pathway via an embedded carbon nanotube (CNT) network for smart-weapon and electronically controlled applications. The paper also addresses, with quantitative grounding where possible, the synthesis and manufacturing scale-up challenges associated with recombinant protein production, and concludes with a detailed financial analysis covering current production cost trajectories, government funding mechanisms (DARPA SBIR/STTR, Army Research Lab, AFWERX), and a technology readiness level (TRL) investment roadmap.

Keywords: caseless ammunition, recombinant spider silk, biopolymer propellant, nitrated poly-amino acid, dual-mode ignition, mechanical striker, electrothermal ignition, cook-off mitigation, GAP energetic polymer, HITP, disulfide cross-linking, DARPA SBIR, technology readiness level

1. Introduction

The metallic cartridge case, brought to functional maturity by the 1870s, has remained the cornerstone of small arms ammunition for over 150 years. This single component — typically a drawn brass cup — simultaneously performs four distinct engineering functions: it seals combustion gases from rearward escape, structurally contains the propellant and projectile during rough field handling, absorbs a significant fraction of post-firing chamber heat via conduction and ejection, and provides a known-geometry surface for the firing pin to act upon. The genius of the design is precisely that one inexpensive stamped part performs all four functions so reliably.

Yet this solution carries a weight penalty that has become operationally consequential. A standard 5.56×45mm NATO cartridge case accounts for approximately 50–60% of the round's total mass. A soldier carrying 300 rounds carries, in effect, 150 rounds worth of inert copper alloy that returns nothing to the mission. The elimination of this mass has been a persistent goal of ammunition designers since World War II, and has become a programmatic priority for the U.S. Army's ongoing Next Generation Squad Weapons (NGSW) effort, which mandated a minimum 40% reduction in ammunition weight relative to legacy 5.56mm.

The two most serious historical attempts at fully caseless ammunition — the Heckler & Koch G11 with its Dynamit Nobel 4.73×33mm DM11 round, and the U.S. Army's Lightweight Small Arms Technologies (LSAT) caseless track — both demonstrated that the concept is technically achievable. The G11K2 was in field trials with the Bundeswehr by 1990, achieving accuracy improvements of at least 50% over the G3 rifle, before German reunification cancelled the programme. Both programmes were ultimately stopped by politics and cost, not physics.

This paper proposes a structurally different approach to the caseless challenge. Rather than treating propellant chemistry as the primary structural element and working backwards to solve its thermal and mechanical weaknesses, the BPC framework begins from a structural material — a cross-linked recombinant protein matrix — and adds energetic chemistry to it. The result is a system where structural integrity, thermal management, weatherproofing, and ignition each have dedicated molecular-level design solutions, rather than competing requirements placed on a single material.

The following sections address: Section 2, relevant prior art; Section 3, the biopolymer casing architecture; Section 4, the nitrated poly-amino acid propellant; Section 5, thermal management; Section 6, the dual-mode ignition system (mechanical primary, electrothermal secondary); Section 7, chamber sealing and weapons integration; Section 8, synthesis and scale-up pathways; Section 9, a financial analysis; and Section 10, conclusions.

2. Prior Art and the State of Caseless Technology

2.1 Historical Programmes

The idea of self-contained ammunition predates the metallic case. Walter Hunt's 1850s Rocket Ball — a hollow-point bullet packed with black powder — was the first commercial caseless design, though its poor ballistic performance precluded adoption. During World War II, material scarcity motivated Germany to develop partially combustible artillery cases, most notably for the 55mm Maschinenkanone MK 155 aircraft autocannon. Japan's Ho-301 40mm autocannon achieved a more complete implementation of the caseless concept with internal-propellant rounds. Post-war, the Soviet GP-25 (1978) and AGS-40 Balkan (2017) grenade launchers represented the most successful fielded internal-propellant caseless systems.

The G11 programme remains the definitive technical reference. Initiated in 1967 by the Gesellschaft für Hülsenlose Gewehrsysteme (GSHG) consortium — H&K (mechanical engineering), Dynamit Nobel (propellant and projectile), and Hensoldt (optics) — the G11 evolved through at least six major prototypes over 23 years. Early prototypes used synthetically bound nitrocellulose blocks and suffered severe cook-off during sustained fire at cyclic rates approaching 2,100 rounds per minute in burst mode. Dynamit Nobel resolved this in the DM11 round using a High Ignition Temperature Propellant (HITP) formulation that raised the spontaneous ignition temperature of the propellant by approximately 100°C above standard nitrocellulose, combined with a heat-resistant polymer binder and lacquer coating. The final round had the projectile fully telescoped within the propellant block in what the programme called a 'telescopic' geometry, improving feed reliability and gas sealing.

The G11K2 ultimately demonstrated the capacity to fire 600 rounds at the same weight as 270 rounds of 5.56mm. Despite being a functional technical success — it outperformed competing designs in ACR programme trials — it never entered production.

2.2 LSAT and Modern Successors

The U.S. LSAT programme (2004–2019) pursued two parallel tracks: cased-telescoped (CT) polymer-cased ammunition and a true caseless design derived from the G11 technology, which was licensed by the Army in 2004. In 2024, five companies — including Textron Systems, FN America, General Dynamics, PCP Tactical, and SIG Sauer — were awarded contracts for the Next Generation Squad Automatic Rifle (NGSAR), reflecting the Army's continued investment in non-conventional ammunition architectures. Textron's CT 6.5mm and 6.8mm candidates remain in active development.

2.3 Electronic Ignition Precedents

The Voere VEC-91 (Austria, ~1991) used electrical ignition via two 15V camera batteries to fire caseless ammunition in a bolt-action sporting rifle. The Remington EtronX (2000) demonstrated electrical priming in a conventional bolt-action at 150V pulse, achieving lock times of approximately 27 microseconds versus 3–4 milliseconds for mechanical systems — a 100-fold improvement. Both systems failed commercially due to cost and reliability concerns. However, the operating principle is robustly validated: the M61 Vulcan 20mm rotary cannon has used electrically-primed ammunition since 1959, and the Abrams M1A2's M256 120mm gun uses electric primers as standard. In 2024, Israel Weapon Industries unveiled the Arbel electromechanical trigger and fire control system for infantry rifles, signalling renewed industrial interest in electronic weapon interfaces.

3. The Biopolymer Casing: Recombinant Protein Matrix

3.1 Spider Silk as Structural Feedstock

Dragline silk from orb-weaving spiders (Nephila clavipes and related genera) is routinely cited as the toughest natural biopolymer. Its major ampullate spidroin (MaSp) proteins combine polyalanine β-sheet crystalline domains — which provide tensile strength approaching 1.75 GPa in natural fibres — with glycine-rich amorphous regions that confer extensibility and toughness. The combination produces a material with a toughness modulus exceeding 150 MJ/m³ in natural dragline fibres, superior to Kevlar (approximately 50 MJ/m³) by this metric.

Direct harvesting of spider silk at scale is precluded by the animals' cannibalistic and territorial behaviour. Recombinant production — expressing spidroin genes in heterologous hosts — is the only viable industrial pathway. Key expression systems include E. coli fed-batch fermentation, transgenic silkworm rearing, yeast fermentation, and plant expression. For the BPC application, the structural requirements are compressive strength, thermal stability, and complete combustibility; biocompatibility requirements applicable to biomedical silk products are irrelevant, significantly relaxing the purification specifications and reducing downstream processing costs.

3.2 Disulfide Cross-Linking Architecture

Natural spidroins are relatively cysteine-poor. The BPC matrix design introduces a synthetic spidroin analogue with elevated cysteine content at engineered positions throughout the repetitive core domain. Upon moulding and UV or catalyst-induced curing, inter-chain disulfide bonds (R–S–S–R, bond dissociation energy ~251 kJ/mol) form a three-dimensional covalent network throughout the matrix. The resulting material is a protein thermoset: mechanically rigid, dimensionally stable under compressive load, and resistant to deformation at elevated temperature — until the bonds are deliberately cleaved.

This cross-linked geometry confers two layered benefits for the BPC application. First, it provides the mechanical robustness necessary to withstand chambering, feeding from a magazine, and rough handling in field conditions — the fragility of unbound nitrocellulose propellant blocks was one of the documented failure modes of the G11's early DM11 ammunition, and the BPC matrix directly addresses this. Second, the endothermic bond cleavage reaction provides a thermal buffer against cook-off, discussed further in Section 5.

3.3 Hydrophobic Surface Functionalisation

Moisture sensitivity in protein-based materials is driven by hydrogen bonding between water molecules and polar backbone amide groups. For the BPC casing, the outermost 50–100 μm of the matrix is designed with an enriched surface concentration of hydrophobic residues — leucine, phenylalanine, isoleucine, and valine — expressed as a contiguous outer domain in the recombinant protein sequence. These residues self-assemble to minimise surface energy, presenting a hydrophobic face to the environment. The result is a natural analogue of the lotus-effect superhydrophobic surfaces achieved in synthetic polymer coatings, without requiring a separate coating step or petroleum-based wax treatment. This is directly analogous to the hydrophobic lacquer coating applied to G11 DM11 rounds, but implemented at the molecular level within the protein primary structure rather than as an additional surface application.

3.4 Manufacturing Pathway

The BPC casing is produced by injection moulding of a protein-resin slurry — a concentrated aqueous or formic acid solution of the recombinant spidroin — into a precision mould cavity. Curing is initiated by UV photopolymerisation or a chemical crosslinking catalyst (e.g. copper-catalysed azide-alkyne cycloaddition for orthogonal crosslinking, or a more conventional oxidative disulfide formation under controlled oxygen partial pressure). The resulting moulded part is dimensionally precise and structurally rigid, analogous to an injection-moulded polymer part but with complete combustibility by design.

This pathway eliminates the multi-stage progressive deep-drawing process required for brass cartridge cases, reducing tool wear and capital intensity. It is directly compatible with high-throughput continuous manufacturing, with cycle times comparable to modern polymer injection moulding processes (seconds to tens of seconds per part). The upstream bioreactor stage is discussed in detail in Section 8.

4. The Energetic Propellant: Nitrated Poly-Amino Acid

4.1 Conventional Propellant Chemistry: Baseline

Standard single-base propellants consist of nitrocellulose (NC) with nitrogen content between 12.6% and 13.8%. The energetic character arises from nitrate ester groups (–ONO₂) on the cellulose backbone; during deflagration, these decompose exothermically to yield predominantly N₂, CO₂, H₂O, and CO, with the ratio of clean products improving as the nitrogen content and oxygen balance are optimised. Double-base propellants add nitroglycerin to NC, increasing energy density at the cost of barrel erosion and heat generation. Modern Improved Military Rifle (IMR) powders achieve specific energies of approximately 3.8–4.2 MJ/kg.

Nitrocellulose's limitation as a caseless propellant binder is its relatively low spontaneous ignition temperature (~170°C) and its tendency to produce a friable, hygroscopic solid when formed into large monolithic blocks — both properties that contributed to the G11's early cook-off problems. The BPC propellant architecture addresses both by replacing the cellulose backbone with a protein backbone and by using a higher-energy nitrogen functionalisation strategy.

4.2 Nitrated Poly-Amino Acid Architecture

The BPC propellant is a synthetic polypeptide backbone in which specific amino acid side chains are post-polymerisation modified with energetic functional groups. The design analogises established nitrogen-rich energetic polymer chemistry — specifically glycidyl azide polymer (GAP) and its derivatives — but applied to a biological backbone.

Arginine residues carry a guanidinium side chain that can be nitrated under controlled conditions to yield guanidine nitrate derivatives, a well-characterised high-nitrogen functional group. Lysine residues carry a terminal ε-amino group susceptible to nitration or azidation. Serine and threonine residues carry hydroxyl groups convertible to nitrate esters (–ONO₂) analogous to the NC modification. By selecting a backbone composition with defined proportions of these residues, the energetic density and oxygen balance of the final propellant can be tuned by adjusting the nitration protocol.

GAP — the most studied azide energetic polymer — has a heat of formation of +117.2 kcal/mol, a density of 1.30 g/cm³, and a high burning rate of 1 cm/s at 40 atmospheres. It is also chemically compatible with a range of explosive fillers including HMX and RDX, and is used as an energetic binder in advanced composite propellants. The nitrated poly-amino acid propellant in the BPC system is designed to achieve comparable energetic performance while offering the additional advantage that its combustion backbone is CHN/O-only (no chlorine, no sulphur, no lead), producing N₂, CO₂, and H₂O as primary combustion products with substantially less barrel fouling and toxicity than lead styphnate-initiated NC propellants.

4.3 Solid Block Geometry and Energy Density

Conventional granular propellant powders pack at approximately 60–70% volumetric efficiency depending on grain geometry, leaving substantial void space between grains. A monolithically cast propellant block eliminates this dead space entirely, allowing the BPC propellant charge to occupy a higher fraction of the cartridge volume. If the propellant achieves specific energy comparable to modern double-base propellants (~4.0 MJ/kg) at a cast density of 1.30 g/cm³, the volumetric energy density is approximately 5.2 MJ/L, roughly 40–50% higher than a typical 5.56mm propellant charge loaded at conventional packing density. This arithmetic underpins the theoretical feasibility of achieving equal or superior muzzle velocity from a smaller and lighter propellant mass, consistent with the G11's demonstrated ballistic performance from a 4.73mm projectile at 3.25g mass.

4.4 Oxygen Balance Tuning

Oxygen balance (Ω%) is defined for an explosive of formula CₐHᵦNᵧOδ as Ω% = 1600(δ − 2α − β/2)/MW, where MW is the molecular weight. A material with Ω = 0 contains precisely the oxygen needed to fully combust its carbon and hydrogen content without external air, yielding clean gaseous products. A negative oxygen balance leaves unburned carbon (soot/fouling); a positive balance generates excess oxygen which may erode the barrel. For a fully consumable caseless propellant, an oxygen balance in the range −5% to +5% is the design target. This can be achieved in the nitrated poly-amino acid system by combining nitrate ester modifications (which are oxygen-positive, like NC's –ONO₂ groups) with azide modifications (which are oxygen-neutral or negative), and adjusting the backbone amino acid composition to set the baseline C/H/N/O ratio before functionalisation. Computational prediction of Ω% for candidate sequences is straightforward using established thermochemical codes (EXPLO5, CHEETAH, ICy), making sequence-level formulation optimisation tractable prior to wet chemistry.

5. Thermal Management: Cook-Off Mitigation Strategy

Cook-off is the failure mode that terminated several caseless ammunition programmes. In the G11, it was ultimately addressed by raising the propellant's spontaneous ignition temperature 100°C above standard NC via HITP chemistry — but this required years of propellant reformulation by Dynamit Nobel. The BPC framework incorporates a multi-layer thermal management strategy, each layer providing incremental protection.

5.1 Layer 1: HITP-Analogous Protein Binder

The cross-linked disulfide protein matrix itself has a spontaneous ignition temperature substantially higher than standard NC. Proteins do not typically undergo exothermic deflagration below 250–300°C under ambient conditions; the ignition temperature of protein-based materials in compression (analogous to a propellant block in a hot chamber) is not a precisely published value for the specific BPC architecture, but is conservatively estimated to exceed the HITP formulation's ~270°C threshold. The protein matrix acts as the first thermal barrier by virtue of its intrinsically higher activation energy for oxidative decomposition.

5.2 Layer 2: Endothermic Disulfide Cleavage

As chamber temperature rises, the disulfide bonds in the cross-linked protein matrix begin to undergo reductive or thermal cleavage before the protein backbone itself reaches combustion temperature. The cleavage enthalpy for a disulfide bond is approximately +251 kJ/mol. At a network density of, say, one disulfide per 500 Da of protein mass, a 5-gram casing contains approximately 10 mmol of disulfide bonds, representing an endothermic energy absorption capacity of approximately 2.5 kJ before significant backbone decomposition begins. While this is a modest absolute figure relative to the total thermal energy generated in a firing cycle (~2 kJ for a 5.56mm round), it represents a meaningful thermal buffer specifically at the propellant–casing interface, which is where cook-off risk is highest. The effect is analogous to a phase-change thermal buffer: energy is absorbed, not released.

5.3 Layer 3: Hydrophobic Ablative Outer Layer

The hydrophobic surface functionalisation described in Section 3.3 also provides an ablative thermal barrier. The higher hydrophobicity of leucine and phenylalanine side chains correlates with increased thermal stability relative to polar residues under oxidative conditions. The outer surface of the BPC casing thus presents a higher effective ignition barrier to radiant heat from the chamber walls than the propellant core, creating a thermal gradient that protects the energetically active interior.

5.4 Weapon-Level Thermal Management

No purely ammunition-based thermal management strategy is sufficient for indefinitely sustained high-rate automatic fire. The BPC weapon system design should incorporate supplementary thermal control at the platform level: ceramic or zirconia-coated chamber walls to reduce heat transfer from the barrel to the chambered round; a low thermal-mass lightweight barrel liner; and cyclic rate governors that enforce a controlled firing pause when a barrel temperature sensor approaches a pre-set threshold. These are standard elements of the thermal design toolkit for sustained-fire weapons and are not novel requirements imposed by the BPC design; they are more tractable in the BPC system because the protein matrix's higher spontaneous ignition temperature provides additional engineering margin relative to standard NC.

6. Dual-Mode Ignition System

The BPC ignition system is designed around a primary mechanical percussion pathway with a secondary electrothermal pathway. This architecture provides full functionality in weapons without electronic infrastructure, while enabling enhanced capabilities — precision firing control, programmable ignition, biometric safeties — in platforms that support it.

6.1 Mechanical Primer: Design Philosophy for Caseless Rounds

In a conventional cased round, the primer is a self-contained sub-assembly: a small brass cup filled with a percussion-sensitive pyrotechnic compound, seated in the base of the cartridge case. When the firing pin strikes the cup, the pyrotechnic compound detonates and the resulting hot gas and flame jet ignites the main propellant charge.

In a fully caseless round, the geometry is different. There is no separate primer cup because there is no case base to seat it in. Three design approaches have been historically explored:

  • External primer: a separate disc-shaped or wafer primer that is loaded alongside the propellant block and consumed during firing (used in some early G11 prototypes with a separate primer cup adjacent to the propellant block);
  • Integrated rear primer: the aft face of the propellant block is formulated with a percussion-sensitive composition at higher concentration than the main charge, so that a firing pin strike on the rear face initiates the burn directly (the approach refined in later DM11 G11 ammunition); and
  • Electrical priming: no mechanical primer, initiation entirely via electric current (Voere VEC-91 approach).

The BPC mechanical primer uses the Integrated Rear Primer approach, adapted for the protein-based architecture. The aft face of the propellant block is impregnated during moulding with a percussion-sensitive energetic compound — a micro-granular mix of lead styphnate or, for a lead-free design, a diazonium-based or tetrazene priming composition — at a surface density sufficient to reliably initiate upon a firing pin strike of 5–10 mJ mechanical energy (the standard threshold for commercial small arms primers). This primer surface is then sealed by a thin (50–100 μm) layer of the hydrophobic protein matrix to protect it from mechanical abrasion during handling, while being thin enough to not impede firing pin penetration.

6.2 Firing Pin Interface: The Sealing Striker

The most critical weapon-system challenge for a caseless round with a mechanical primer is the firing pin's relationship to the chamber seal. In a cased round, the cartridge case performs the gas seal at the breech face; the firing pin passes through a small hole in the bolt face and does not need to participate in sealing. In a caseless system, the bolt face itself must seal the aft end of the chamber against combustion pressure (typically 60,000–70,000 psi in a 5.56mm-class round).

The G11 addressed this with a rotating cylindrical chamber (the 'rotary breech') that sealed both the front and rear faces of the propellant block within the chamber bore before firing, without requiring the bolt face to provide the primary gas seal. The BPC system proposes a simpler adaptation: a recessed bolt face with a conical or hemispherical striker probe that seats against the aft face of the BPC round's polymer protein matrix when the bolt closes. When the round is chambered and the bolt is in battery, the annular contact surface between the bolt face's recessed rim and the aft face of the casing provides the primary gas seal, relying on the elastic compliance of the protein matrix surface under bolt closure force to create a gas-tight mating surface.

This is not an unprecedented sealing approach — rotating bolt gas sealing against a combustible case base has been demonstrated in Metal Storm stacked-round systems and in certain artillery propellant bag designs — but it places tolerance requirements on the casing's aft face that are tighter than those for conventional polymer-cased ammunition. The protein matrix's injection-moulded origin provides, in principle, dimensional precision comparable to precision-moulded polymer parts (tolerances of ±0.02 mm routinely achievable), making this engineering challenge tractable.

6.3 Firing Pin Geometry and Kinetic Energy Requirements

The firing pin in a BPC weapon is a striker probe approximately 1.2–1.5 mm in diameter and 2–3 mm in stroke travel, spring-loaded to approximately 8–12 J of kinetic energy at impact. This is consistent with standard striker-fired pistol and rifle firing pin specifications. Upon impact with the aft face of the BPC round, the striker breaks through the thin protective protein seal layer and impacts the percussion-sensitive primer surface. The primer initiates and the flame jet propagates into the rear of the propellant block.

Because the BPC casing's aft face is a moulded protein matrix rather than a brass cup, there is no risk of 'primer flow' (the deformation and rearward extrusion of brass primer material into the firing pin hole under high pressure, a known failure mode in overpressure conditions with conventional brass cases). The protein matrix instead ablates cleanly. The absence of an ejectable metallic primer cup — one of the last non-consumable components in the G11's DM11 round — means the BPC system achieves truly zero-residue firing.

6.4 Electrothermal Secondary Pathway: Carbon Nanotube Network

The secondary ignition pathway is electrothermal, initiated by Joule heating through a conductive single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT) network embedded in the aft 1–2 mm of the propellant block, at the same radial position as the percussion-sensitive primer surface. Individual SWCNTs exhibit electrical conductivities exceeding 10⁶ S/m. The embedded network is configured to present a controlled resistance of approximately 5–10 Ω to the weapon's firing circuit. A brief current pulse (5–15 A for 1–5 ms) from a bolt-integrated electrode contact — driven by a weapon-mounted battery and discharge capacitor — delivers Q = I²Rt joules of heat, where Q for a 10 A pulse over 2 ms through a 7 Ω network is approximately 1.4 J, sufficient to raise the primer surface temperature well above the initiation threshold.

This secondary pathway adds no moving parts. It does require two electrode contacts on the bolt face (insulated from the casing body) and a power supply in the weapon. It enables: (i) electronic firing control with sub-millisecond lock time; (ii) programmable inhibit/permit based on biometric authorisation; (iii) integration with smart weapon fire control processors; and (iv) the ability to selectively fire in scenarios where mechanical firing pin impact would generate unacceptable acoustic signatures (hypothetical directed-energy context). In its simplest implementation — a 9V battery and a capacitor, analogous to the Remington EtronX architecture — it adds minimal weight and complexity to the weapon.

6.5 Mode Selection and Redundancy

The two ignition pathways are mutually compatible and do not interfere. A weapon fielded without electronic firing capability operates entirely via the mechanical striker, with the CNT network present but inactive. A weapon with electronic firing capability can operate in mechanical-primary mode (CNT network as backup) or electronic-primary mode (mechanical striker as failsafe if electronics fail). This redundancy architecture is a significant operational advantage over the all-or-nothing electronic-only design of the Voere VEC-91 and Remington EtronX, both of which lost all firing capability when their battery systems failed.

7. Chamber Sealing and Weapons Integration

Chamber sealing without a metallic case is the most frequently cited objection to caseless ammunition in mainstream weapons engineering literature. The G11's solution — a rotating cylindrical chamber that sealed all four faces of the square-section propellant block within the chamber bore — required the most mechanically complex rifle ever seriously fielded, described by critics as 'the inside of a compact clock.' A simpler sealing solution is a prerequisite for any caseless system intended for wide adoption.

The BPC system's approach is described in Section 6.2. The key design insight is that the protein matrix's compliance under load — unlike a rigid brass case, the protein casing can elastically deform at the bolt-face contact surface — allows the bolt's annular rim to form a dynamic gas seal against the casing without requiring the precision rotating geometry of the G11. This is similar in principle to how a polymer-cased telescoped (CT) round achieves gas sealing in Textron's LSAT CT weapons: the polymer case base provides a compliant seal against the bolt face, and the case does not need to expand and contract like brass to perform this function.

For the forward (muzzle) end of the chamber, the BPC round's projectile seats in the rifled bore with a driving band that provides gas sealing in the conventional manner. The propellant block behind it is contained within the chamber walls. The absence of any extractable case means the BPC weapon's ejection port can be eliminated entirely — as in the G11 — simplifying the receiver geometry and providing a completely sealed action less susceptible to dust and debris ingestion.

BPC weapon design should adopt a rotary bolt lockup (the standard in most military rifles) or a straight-pull bolt, both compatible with the sealing strategy described. A short-stroke gas piston or long-stroke gas operation provides the cyclic energy to chamber the next round and reset the firing mechanism. The overall action is simpler than the G11's by virtue of eliminating extraction, ejection, and the rotary chamber, and is broadly compatible with the operating principles of existing gas-operated military rifle designs.

8. Synthesis and Scale-Up: Challenges and Strategies

The most significant near-term barrier to BPC system realisation is not physics — it is biology and manufacturing. Producing recombinant spider silk proteins in the quantities required for an ammunition programme, at a cost structure compatible with military procurement, requires advances beyond the current state of art. This section surveys the challenge quantitatively and identifies the most credible pathways to solution.

8.1 Production Quantity Requirements

Consider a mid-scale military procurement: 100 million rounds per year, each containing approximately 3–5 grams of protein matrix (casing) and 2–3 grams of nitrated propellant precursor protein. The casing protein alone requires 300,000–500,000 kg of recombinant spidroin per year. For comparison, the global synthetic spider silk market was valued at USD 160 million in 2024, and total global production capacity across all companies is estimated at hundreds of kilograms to low metric-tonne scale. A BPC ammunition programme at military scale would require approximately a 1,000-fold increase in production capacity relative to the current commercial baseline.

This sounds formidable, but is not unprecedented in the history of industrial biotechnology. Recombinant insulin, now produced at multi-tonne scale globally from E. coli and S. cerevisiae fermentation, required a similarly radical scale-up from laboratory demonstration to industrial production over approximately 15–20 years from first demonstration (1978) to global availability. Recombinant citric acid from Aspergillus niger grew from pilot scale to multi-million-tonne annual production over a similar timeframe. The BPC programme requires the same trajectory for spider silk proteins.

8.2 Expression Systems and Yield Benchmarks

Current leading yield benchmarks for recombinant spidroin production in E. coli fed-batch fermentation are approximately 14–20 g/L of purified protein after downstream processing — a result achieved and published by Schacht et al. and independently replicated, representing a roughly 10-fold improvement over the 1–2 g/L yields that characterised the field a decade ago. Spiber's pilot-scale fermentation facility in Thailand, using genetically engineered yeast, achieves production at 150 m³ bioreactor scale and is targeting commercial deliveries in late 2025 at a reported 500-tonne annual capacity for its first commercial plant.

For BPC purposes, the protein does not need to meet biomedical purity specifications (endotoxin levels, sterility, etc.), substantially simplifying downstream processing. The primary quality requirements are structural — molecular weight distribution, degree of polymerisation, presence of cysteine residues for crosslinking — plus energetic chemistry requirements for the propellant fraction. Removing biomedical quality constraints could reduce downstream processing cost by 30–50%, based on analogies with industrial enzyme production versus pharmaceutical protein production.

8.3 Transgenic Silkworm Platform

Kraig Biocraft Laboratories' transgenic silkworm platform offers a cost advantage of approximately one order of magnitude relative to E. coli vat fermentation: the company reports production costs below USD 300/kg, compared to estimated USD 2,000–5,000/kg for fermentation-derived recombinant silk. Kraig is targeting 10 metric tonnes of spider silk production in 2025, with infrastructure expansion ongoing across Southeast Asia. In March 2025, the company produced its largest single batch in company history. Critically, Kraig has received U.S. Army funding for ballistic materials applications — directly adjacent to the BPC use case — and registered the SpydaSilk trademark in 2025 for high-performance fibre applications.

The silkworm platform scales with mulberry cultivation area and silkworm rearing capacity, both of which are well-understood agricultural systems in Southeast Asia. Kraig has taken possession of strategic mulberry fields and additional rearing centres through early 2026, signalling active preparation for significant production expansion. At USD 300/kg and 3–5 g/round casing mass, the silk protein input cost per round is approximately USD 0.09–0.15 — competitive with or below the material cost of a standard brass cartridge case.

8.4 Overcoming the Molecular Weight Challenge

Full-length natural spidroins have molecular weights of 200–350 kDa, but are notoriously difficult to express in heterologous hosts at full length due to repetitive sequences that cause ribosomal slippage and premature translation termination. Industrial approaches to this problem include: (i) using truncated spidroin analogues that retain the key structural properties at reduced molecular weight (60–100 kDa), as successfully demonstrated by Schacht et al. with their miniature spidroin design; (ii) intein-mediated ligation of expressed shorter fragments into full-length proteins post-translationally; and (iii) directed evolution of expression hosts for improved tolerance of repetitive mRNA sequences. For the BPC application, the molecular weight requirement is less stringent than for fibre applications (which require long-chain proteins for fibre drawing), because the casing is moulded rather than spun. Truncated spidroins at 60–80 kDa with adequate cysteine content for crosslinking may be entirely sufficient.

8.5 Nitration Scale-Up

Post-polymerisation nitration of the propellant protein presents its own scale-up challenges. Industrial nitration of cellulose (to produce NC) is a mature continuous process performed at multi-tonne scale using mixed nitric/sulphuric acid. Nitration of protein side chains under controlled conditions is chemically analogous, though the structural fragility of protein backbones under strongly acidic conditions requires milder nitration protocols or selective chemical modifications. Arginine guanidinium nitration and lysine azidation have been demonstrated in academic chemistry contexts; scale-up to tonne quantities would require process development for controlled reaction selectivity, residual acid removal, and safe handling of the energetic product. This is an engineering challenge rather than a fundamental scientific barrier — one similar to those routinely managed in bulk energetic materials manufacturing — but it requires significant investment in process chemistry and explosion-proof infrastructure.

8.6 Energetic Materials Regulatory and Infrastructure Requirements

Energetic materials manufacturing is subject to regulatory oversight by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) in the U.S., the Defence Science and Technology Group (DSTG) in Australia, and equivalent bodies internationally. Production facilities require ATF licences (manufacturers licence Type 20 for explosive manufacturing), appropriate site separation distances, and compliance with OSHA Process Safety Management standards for explosive chemical manufacture. These requirements are well-defined and routinely met by existing propellant manufacturers; a new BPC production facility would follow the same compliance pathway, with no novel regulatory categories required.

9. Financial Analysis: Cost Structure, Funding, and Investment Roadmap

9.1 Global Market Context

The global caseless ammunition market was valued at USD 1.21 billion in 2024 and is projected to grow at a CAGR of 7.8% through 2033, reaching approximately USD 2.41 billion. North America accounts for approximately 38% of this market (USD 460 million in 2024), driven by U.S. Army modernisation programmes. Asia-Pacific is the fastest-growing segment at 9.2% CAGR, driven by defence modernisation in China, India, South Korea, and Australia. These figures represent the total addressable market (TAM) for a fully developed BPC system, noting that BPC technology would compete within this space against Textron CT ammunition and other advanced designs.

The synthetic spider silk market, which provides the core input material, was valued at USD 160 million in 2024 and is projected to reach USD 2.58 billion by 2025–2035 at a CAGR of approximately 13–16% depending on the forecast methodology. The global technical fibres market — the relevant comparator for defence applications — was valued at approximately USD 206 billion in 2024. Spider silk's entry into the ballistic and technical fibre markets, actively pursued by Kraig Biocraft with U.S. Army funding, is the precursor market that directly enables BPC material supply.

9.2 Per-Round Cost Projections

A preliminary per-round cost decomposition for a BPC 5.56mm-class round in a mature production scenario:

__Protein casing material (spider silk, ~4g at USD 300/kg): __USD 0.12

__Nitration reagents and propellant processing (estimated at 2× NC processing cost): __USD 0.08–0.12

__Projectile (standard lead-core or solid copper, 62 gr): __USD 0.15–0.18

__CNT ignition layer (SWCNT at projected volume pricing, ~0.1 mg/round): __USD 0.02–0.05

__Moulding, curing, and assembly labour + overhead: __USD 0.08–0.15

__Estimated total per round (mature production): __USD 0.45–0.62

For comparison, standard M855A1 5.56mm Enhanced Performance Round procurement costs the U.S. Army approximately USD 0.37–0.45 per round at recent contract prices. The BPC round in initial production will be significantly more expensive — potentially USD 1.50–3.00 during Phase I/II development phases — but the mature production cost projection is within approximately 30–50% of current M855A1 pricing, a premium the military routinely pays for capability improvements (the steel-core M855A1 itself was significantly more expensive than its M855 predecessor upon introduction).

The critical cost driver that narrows this gap over time is spider silk protein production cost, which is currently falling along a steep experience curve. Kraig Biocraft's transgenic silkworm platform at USD 300/kg is already 10× cheaper than fermentation-based competitors, and Kraig has stated its production cost advantage over fermentation is approximately one order of magnitude. Further scale-up to 1,000+ tonne annual production is expected to drive costs below USD 100/kg within 5–10 years, which would reduce the casing protein cost to under USD 0.04 per round — well below the brass case cost of a standard M855A1 cartridge (approximately USD 0.08–0.12 per round at bulk military pricing).

9.3 Government Funding Pathways

DARPA SBIR/STTR

The Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency SBIR programme is the most directly applicable early-stage funding mechanism for the BPC system. DARPA's SBIR has an explicit culture of tolerance for high-risk, high-reward proposals and actively seeks technology that 'reaches beyond current military capabilities.' DARPA SBIR Phase I awards are typically USD 250,000 over 6 months for feasibility assessment. Phase II awards typically reach USD 1.5–2.0 million over 24 months. Direct to Phase II (DP2) awards, available for topics with strong preliminary data, can reach USD 3.5 million. A BPC Phase I SBIR would target feasibility demonstration of (i) the recombinant casing protein synthesis and crosslinking and (ii) small-scale synthesis of the nitrated propellant precursor. DARPA's Biological Technologies Office (BTO) and Defence Sciences Office (DSO) both have portfolios relevant to BPC: BTO for the recombinant protein manufacturing track, DSO for the energetic materials and weapons integration track.

Army Research Laboratory (ARL) and CCDC AC

The Army Combat Capabilities Development Command, Armaments Center (CCDC AC) at Picatinny Arsenal, New Jersey, is the principal Army organisation responsible for ammunition research and development. CCDC AC funds programmes directly through Other Transaction Authority (OTA) agreements and Congressional appropriations, with programme values typically in the USD 5–25 million range per multi-year effort for advanced ammunition technologies. A BPC development programme would be structured as a Technology Maturation Initiative (TMI) or Prototype Project under OTA, with milestones tied to Technology Readiness Level (TRL) advancement.

AFWERX and Multi-Service Programmes

AFWERX, the Air Force innovation arm, funds dual-use technology with both Air Force and broader DoD relevance. The electronic ignition pathway of the BPC system — directly applicable to aircraft-mounted weapons — is within AFWERX's portfolio scope. SOCOM's Command, Control, Communications and Computers office has historically funded novel small arms technology through similar mechanisms. A multi-service funding strategy that argues the BPC system simultaneously addresses Army weight reduction goals, Air Force smart-weapon integration objectives, and SOCOM operational requirements would maximise the addressable funding pool and reduce dependence on any single programme.

Allied Defence Funding

For an Australian-developed BPC programme, the Defence Innovation Hub (DIH) and Next Generation Technologies Fund (NGTF) under the Australian Department of Defence provide the primary grant mechanisms, with awards typically ranging from AUD 250,000 (DIH Phase 1) to AUD 10+ million (NGTF major grants). Australia's access to the Five Eyes science and technology sharing framework — and the AUKUS trilateral partnership — creates a direct pathway to U.S. DoD co-investment once proof-of-concept is demonstrated at TRL 3–4 in Australia, a far shorter route to U.S. programme entry than for a company without allied nation status.

9.4 Technology Readiness Level Roadmap and Investment Profile

The following TRL roadmap is proposed for BPC system development:

__TRL 1–2 (Basic Research, ~AUD 500K–1.5M, 12–18 months): __Synthesis and structural characterisation of candidate cysteine-rich recombinant spidroin constructs; DSC thermal analysis of disulfide cleavage energy; synthesis of model nitrated polypeptide compounds and measurement of detonation velocity and burn rate; computational oxygen balance prediction. Funding source: Australian DIH Phase 1, university collaborative grants, DARPA SBIR Phase I.

__TRL 3–4 (Proof of Concept, ~USD 3–8M, 24 months): __Fabrication and mechanical testing of moulded protein casing prototypes under compressive loading and thermal cycling; small-scale energetic propellant block synthesis and burn testing in a confined cell; demonstration of mechanical primer initiation on a protein matrix substrate; first-article CNT ignition layer testing. Funding source: DARPA SBIR Phase II, ARL direct award, NGTF major grant.

__TRL 5–6 (Component Validation, ~USD 15–40M, 36 months): __Full BPC round assembly and static firing in a proof-of-concept weapon; muzzle velocity, pressure, and fouling measurements; cook-off threshold testing; 1,000-round endurance trial in a prototype weapon. Funding source: CCDC AC OTA, Congressional plus-up, Five Eyes co-investment.

__TRL 7–9 (System Demonstration and Production, ~USD 50–200M, 60 months+): __Weapon system development and qualification; 100,000-round fielding trial; pilot production facility establishment (~100-tonne annual protein capacity); Army/Allied Forces type classification. Funding source: DoD programme of record, prime defence contractor partnership.

9.5 Intellectual Property and Commercialisation

The BPC framework, as described, represents a novel combination of individually published elements in a previously undescribed integrated architecture. Key patentable elements include: (i) the specific recombinant spidroin sequence design with elevated cysteine density and hydrophobic surface domain; (ii) the cross-linked protein matrix as a caseless ammunition structural element; (iii) the dual-mode mechanical/CNT ignition system for a consumable round; (iv) the nitrated poly-amino acid propellant formulation methodology; and (v) the process for manufacturing BPC rounds via injection moulding and UV crosslinking. Filing provisional patents covering these claims at TRL 2 is advisable before any publication or government contract submission, to establish priority dates in the U.S. (USPTO), Australia (IP Australia), and relevant allied jurisdictions. A defensible patent portfolio is a prerequisite for negotiating licensing terms with prime contractors and for attracting private investment co-investment alongside government funding.

The dual-use commercialisation story — the same recombinant protein production infrastructure that feeds a BPC ammunition programme also has commercial applications in ballistic materials (spider silk body armour is an active research and commercial area, with Kraig Biocraft and Warwick Mills active in this space), high-performance textiles, and biomedical scaffolds — strengthens the investment case for early-stage funders and positions any equity component of the funding structure to benefit from multiple exit pathways beyond the core defence programme.

10. Discussion

The BPC framework synthesises four domains — structural biopolymers, energetic polymer chemistry, caseless weapon design, and electronic/mechanical ignition systems — into an integrated ammunition architecture. Each subsystem has published, demonstrable precedents. The novel contribution is the integration: no prior work has proposed using a cross-linked recombinant protein as the structural casing element, nor has the combination of a nitrated biological backbone with a dual mechanical/electrothermal CNT ignition been described.

The most important distinction between the BPC design and prior caseless approaches is the separation of structural and energetic functions. In the G11's DM11 round and in most prior caseless designs, the propellant block must simultaneously provide structural integrity, weatherproofing, thermal protection, and energetic output — four competing requirements in a single material, each of which imposes constraints on the others. The BPC system uses the protein matrix for structure, weatherproofing, and partial thermal management, while delegating energetics to the nitrated propellant compound. This functional separation allows each material to be independently optimised.

The dual-mode ignition system represents a genuine operational improvement over single-mode caseless designs. Both the Voere VEC-91 and the Remington EtronX — the two historical civilian implementations of electrical ignition — failed partly because they had no fallback when their electronic systems malfunctioned. A BPC weapon with a mechanical-primary, electronic-secondary architecture is operationally resilient in a way neither of those systems was.

The main acknowledged uncertainties at this stage of conceptual development are: the actual spontaneous ignition temperature of the nitrated poly-amino acid propellant in its cast form; the mechanical strength of the crosslinked protein matrix under the shear and compressive loads imposed by chambering in a dirty field environment; the CNT network's resistance to static discharge at all relevant environmental temperatures and humidity levels; and the precise oxygen balance achievable in the nitrated propellant at practical synthesis conditions. These are all empirically testable at TRL 2–3 and none of them represents a fundamental physical barrier to the concept.

11. Conclusion

This paper has presented the Biopolymere Caseless (BPC) system — a theoretical but technically coherent architecture for fully consumable small arms ammunition integrating: a disulfide-crosslinked recombinant spider silk protein structural casing; a nitrated poly-amino acid energetic propellant formulated to near-zero oxygen balance; a multi-layer cook-off mitigation strategy combining HITP-analogous protein chemistry, endothermic disulfide cleavage, and weapon-level thermal management; a mechanical percussion primary ignition system using an integrated rear primer surface compatible with standard striker-fired weapons; and a secondary electrothermal ignition pathway via an embedded CNT network for smart-weapon applications.

The paper has further characterised the synthesis and manufacturing scale-up pathway, identifying Kraig Biocraft's transgenic silkworm platform at USD 300/kg and Spiber's 500-tonne fermentation facility as the most credible near-term production enablers, and projecting a mature per-round material cost competitive with current M855A1 procurement pricing within a reasonable scale-up horizon. A TRL-structured investment roadmap has been proposed, covering USD 500K–200M+ in phased government and private funding across a 10–15 year development timeline, with DARPA SBIR, CCDC AC OTA, and Australian DIH/NGTF as the primary early-stage funding mechanisms.

The BPC system does not resolve all challenges associated with caseless ammunition in a single conceptual step. Chamber sealing, large-scale energetic materials handling, and weapon system qualification each require substantial engineering work. But the framework is scientifically coherent, the enabling technologies exist or are in active development, and the potential operational benefit — a fully consumable, lead-free, low-fouling ammunition system at competitive per-round cost — justifies the investment in developing it further.

The authors recommend immediate next steps: (i) provisional patent filings covering the key claims; (ii) targeted literature review and computational screening of candidate nitrated polypeptide sequences for oxygen balance and detonation velocity; (iii) preparation of a DARPA SBIR white paper for submission under the Biological Technologies Office; and (iv) engagement with Kraig Biocraft Laboratories and/or the CCDC AC Armaments Center to discuss collaborative research interest.
