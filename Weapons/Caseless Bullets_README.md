## 1. What is the BPC System?

The **Biopolymere Caseless (BPC) system** is a theoretical design for a new type of small arms ammunition that has no metal case and no ejectable residue — every part of the round except the bullet is consumed when it fires.

Traditional bullets work like this:
- A brass cup (the **case**) holds everything together
- Inside the case sits gunpowder (the **propellant**)
- At the base of the case is a small percussion cap (the **primer**)
- A bullet sits in the mouth of the case

When you fire, the firing pin strikes the primer, which ignites the propellant, which pushes the bullet down the barrel. The empty brass case ejects out the side of the gun.

**The BPC round has no brass case.** Instead:
- The structural body of the round is made from an **engineered protein** — essentially a synthetic spider silk
- The propellant is a **protein that has been chemically modified to be explosive**
- The primer is **built directly into the rear face** of the round
- Everything except the bullet **burns completely** when fired

> **In plain terms:** imagine a self-contained block that is made of material that functions as both the bullet's container AND the gunpowder, burns away entirely, and leaves nothing behind.

## 2. Why Caseless Ammunition?

### The Weight Problem

A soldier carrying 300 rounds of standard 5.56mm NATO ammunition is carrying roughly **half that weight in brass** — the cases. The brass does its job and gets thrown on the ground. It contributes nothing to the mission after it leaves the gun.

| Cartridge component | % of total round weight |
|---|---|
| Projectile (the bullet) | ~20% |
| Propellant | ~18% |
| Primer compound | ~1% |
| Brass case | ~55–60% |

Eliminate the case, and a soldier can carry roughly **twice as many rounds for the same weight**, or carry the same number of rounds and free up weight for other equipment.

### The Mechanical Simplification

A caseless weapon has no extraction cycle — there is no empty case to pull out and eject. This means:
- No extractor claw
- No ejection port (a significant weak point for dirt and debris entry)
- Fewer moving parts
- Potentially higher cyclic rates

### Combat Load Example

The Heckler & Koch G11 caseless rifle, the most advanced caseless weapon ever developed, demonstrated that 600 rounds of its 4.73mm caseless ammunition weighed the same as **270 rounds** of standard 5.56mm NATO. A soldier with a G11 and a full combat load of ammo had more than twice the firepower per kilogram of ammunition carried.

## 3. The Problem With Every Caseless Design Before This One

Every caseless design in history ran into the same four problems:

### Problem 1: Cook-Off
A brass cartridge case acts as a heat sink. When you fire rapidly, each ejected case carries away some of the heat that built up in the chamber. Without a case, that heat stays in the gun. Eventually the chamber gets hot enough to **fire the next round without pulling the trigger**. This is called cook-off, and it is dangerous and potentially lethal.

The G11 programme spent years reformulating its propellant to solve this. Early G11 prototypes cooked off regularly during sustained automatic fire.

### Problem 2: Fragility
A block of solid propellant is brittle. Drop a magazine, bump a round against a hard surface, subject it to the vibrations of field use — the propellant block can crack. A cracked round feeds unreliably and can burn unpredictably.

### Problem 3: Moisture
Solid propellant absorbs moisture. Humidity, rain, and condensation can degrade the propellant's performance or prevent ignition entirely. Conventional brass cases are sealed and waterproof. Bare propellant blocks are not.

### Problem 4: Chamber Sealing
The brass case expands when fired and seals the chamber, preventing hot combustion gases from venting backwards into the mechanism. Without a case, you need another way to seal the chamber. The G11 used a rotating cylindrical chamber — an extraordinarily complex mechanism described by critics as "the inside of a clock." That complexity made the G11 difficult to maintain and expensive to produce.

### Why All Prior Solutions Were Compromises

Prior caseless designs tried to solve all four problems with one material — the propellant block itself. The block had to be:
- Mechanically strong (fragility problem)
- Moisture-resistant (weatherproofing problem)
- Heat-resistant up to a point (cook-off problem)
- Yet also burn completely and reliably on ignition

These requirements fight each other. Making the propellant harder makes it more brittle. Making it moisture-resistant changes its burn chemistry. The BPC design solves this by **separating the functions across different materials**, each optimised for its specific job.

## 4. BPC System Overview — How It All Fits Together

The BPC round has four distinct functional layers:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  PROJECTILE                                     │
│  Standard copper-jacketed or solid copper bullet│
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  PROPELLANT BLOCK                               │
│  Nitrated poly-amino acid — the "fuel"          │
│  Cast as a high-density monolithic solid        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  STRUCTURAL CASING (wraps the propellant block) │
│  Cross-linked recombinant spider silk protein   │
│  Provides strength, weatherproofing, heat buffer│
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  AFT FACE (base of the round)                   │
│  Percussion-sensitive primer surface (mechanical│
│  ignition) + embedded CNT network (electronic  │
│  ignition) — fully consumable, no brass cup    │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

When fired:
1. The firing pin strikes the aft face
2. The percussion primer initiates
3. The flame jet ignites the propellant block
4. The propellant block burns completely
5. The casing burns with it
6. The only thing that leaves the barrel is the bullet

## 5. Component 1: The Protein Casing

### What It Is

The casing is made from a **recombinant spider silk protein** that has been engineered beyond natural spider silk. It is not woven or spun — it is moulded into shape like a plastic part.

### Why Spider Silk?

Spider dragline silk is the toughest natural material on Earth by the metric of **toughness modulus** — a combined measure of strength and extensibility. To put it in perspective:

| Material | Toughness modulus |
|---|---|
| High-tensile steel | ~6 MJ/m³ |
| Kevlar | ~50 MJ/m³ |
| Natural spider dragline silk | ~150+ MJ/m³ |

It is also made entirely of carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and oxygen — the same elements as gunpowder. So unlike a polymer plastic or a ceramic, **spider silk can burn completely** when exposed to sufficient heat. This is essential for a zero-residue caseless round.

### The Engineering: Disulfide Cross-Linking

Natural spider silk proteins do not form a rigid solid on their own — they form fibres. For the BPC casing we need a rigid moulded shape, like a plastic part. This is achieved by engineering the protein to include many **cysteine** amino acid residues throughout its structure.

Cysteine residues form **disulfide bonds** between protein chains — chemical bridges written as R–S–S–R. When these form throughout the protein matrix during the curing process, they create a three-dimensional web of covalent connections, converting the protein from a flexible fibre-forming material into a rigid cross-linked solid. This is the same basic principle as rubber vulcanisation, but in protein chemistry.

The result:
- Mechanically rigid — resists deformation under handling loads
- Dimensionally stable — holds its moulded shape
- Thermally resistant — the disulfide network requires significant heat energy to break

### The Engineering: Hydrophobic Surface Layer

The outermost surface of the casing is designed with a high concentration of **hydrophobic amino acids**: leucine, phenylalanine, isoleucine, and valine. These residues have side chains that repel water. When they are concentrated at the surface, they create a self-assembling water-repellent layer — similar to how a lotus leaf sheds water, but built into the molecular structure of the protein itself.

This solves the moisture problem without requiring any coating, wax treatment, or post-processing step. The weatherproofing is inherent to the material.

### Specs

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Base material | Recombinant spidroin (spider silk protein) |
| Key modification | Elevated cysteine residue concentration for disulfide crosslinking |
| Surface treatment | Hydrophobic amino acid surface domain (leucine/phenylalanine-rich) |
| Crosslink type | Disulfide bonds (R–S–S–R), bond energy ~251 kJ/mol |
| Processing | Injection moulded from aqueous protein slurry, UV or catalyst-cured |
| Dimensional tolerance | ±0.02 mm (comparable to precision-moulded polymers) |
| Combustibility | Complete — C, H, N, O backbone burns to CO₂, H₂O, N₂ |
| Approximate casing mass per round | 3–5 g |

## 6. Component 2: The Propellant

### What It Is

The propellant is a **solid block of modified protein** that has been chemically treated to make it explosive. Think of it as a biological analogue of nitrocellulose (smokeless powder) — but the backbone is an amino acid chain instead of a cellulose chain.

### Background: How Conventional Propellants Work

Standard smokeless powder (nitrocellulose, or NC) is made by treating cellulose — wood pulp — with nitric acid. The acid replaces hydroxyl groups on the cellulose with nitrate ester groups (–ONO₂). When ignited, these nitrogen–oxygen groups decompose rapidly, releasing a large amount of energy as heat and gas. The gas expands and pushes the bullet down the barrel.

The key chemistry: the molecule contains its own oxygen supply, so it doesn't need air to burn — it works in the sealed chamber of a gun. This is called being **oxygen-balanced**.

### The BPC Approach: Nitrated Poly-Amino Acid

Instead of starting with cellulose, the BPC propellant starts with a **synthetic polypeptide** — a chain of amino acids, like a simplified protein. After the chain is assembled, specific amino acid side chains are chemically modified to carry energetic functional groups:

- **Arginine** side chains (guanidinium group) are nitrated to guanidine nitrate derivatives — high-nitrogen, energetic
- **Lysine** side chains (terminal amine group) are modified with azide groups (–N₃), similar to the chemistry in glycidyl azide polymer (GAP), a well-characterised military energetic binder
- **Serine/threonine** side chains (hydroxyl groups) are converted to nitrate esters (–ONO₂), directly analogous to the NC modification

The result is a biological backbone carrying the same types of energetic functional groups that make synthetic propellants work — but derived from amino acid chemistry rather than petrochemistry, and combusting with significantly cleaner exhaust.

### Oxygen Balance

**Oxygen balance (Ω%)** is the single most important number for a propellant formulation. It measures whether the material contains the right amount of oxygen to completely burn itself.

The formula is:
```
Ω% = 1600 × (δ − 2α − β/2) / MW
```
Where:
- α = number of carbon atoms in the molecule
- β = number of hydrogen atoms
- δ = number of oxygen atoms
- MW = molecular weight

| Ω% value | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ω > 0 | Too much oxygen — excess O₂ erodes the barrel |
| Ω ≈ 0 | Perfect — complete combustion, no fouling, no excess oxygen |
| Ω < 0 | Not enough oxygen — produces carbon soot (barrel fouling) |

The target for the BPC propellant is **Ω between −5% and +5%**. This is achieved by tuning the ratio of nitrate ester modifications (oxygen-rich) versus azide modifications (oxygen-neutral) versus the baseline amino acid backbone composition.

### Why a Solid Block?

Conventional granular propellant packs at about 60–70% density — there is air between the grains. A monolithic cast block is 100% dense. At the same propellant mass, the block occupies less volume inside the cartridge. At the same cartridge volume, the block allows more propellant mass. Either way, the volumetric energy density is higher.

At a cast density of ~1.30 g/cm³ (comparable to GAP binder) and a specific energy of ~4.0 MJ/kg, the block achieves approximately **5.2 MJ/L volumetric energy density** — roughly 40–50% higher than standard granular 5.56mm propellant at conventional packing density.

### Combustion Products

| Product | Notes |
|---|---|
| N₂ gas | Main nitrogen product — clean, inert |
| CO₂ gas | Main carbon product — clean |
| H₂O vapour | Main hydrogen product — clean |
| CO (trace) | Minor product if Ω slightly negative |
| No lead compounds | Lead styphnate primer eliminated |
| No heavy residue | Compared to traditional gunpowder: dramatically reduced fouling |

### Specs

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Base material | Synthetic polypeptide backbone |
| Energetic modifications | Nitrated arginine, azidated lysine, nitrate ester serine/threonine |
| Density | ~1.30 g/cm³ (cast solid) |
| Specific energy target | ~4.0 MJ/kg |
| Volumetric energy density target | ~5.2 MJ/L |
| Oxygen balance target | −5% to +5% |
| Primary combustion products | N₂, CO₂, H₂O |
| Approximate propellant mass per round (5.56 class) | 2–3 g |

## 7. Component 3: Thermal Management

### The Cook-Off Problem in Detail

When a gun fires, the propellant combustion releases heat. Some of this heat goes into the barrel, some into the bolt, and in a conventional system some is carried away by the ejected brass case. When firing rapidly:
- Each round leaves the hot barrel still slightly cooler than the previous one
- But the chamber is getting progressively hotter
- If the chamber reaches the propellant's spontaneous ignition temperature, the next round fires without a trigger pull

Standard NC propellant ignites spontaneously at around **170°C**. High-Ignition-Temperature Propellant (HITP), developed by Dynamit Nobel for the G11, raised this to approximately **270°C** — buying 100°C of thermal margin. The BPC system uses a layered approach to achieve similar or better thermal protection.

### Layer 1: Intrinsically High Ignition Temperature

Proteins do not typically deflagrate below 250–300°C under ambient conditions. The BPC propellant's protein backbone inherits this higher base ignition temperature relative to standard NC, providing the first thermal margin before any other mechanism is needed.

### Layer 2: Endothermic Disulfide Cleavage

This is the clever part. As the chamber temperature rises toward the ignition threshold, the disulfide bonds in the structural casing begin to break. Breaking these bonds is **endothermic** — it absorbs heat rather than releasing it.

Each disulfide bond requires approximately 251 kJ/mol to cleave. A 5-gram BPC casing with high disulfide crosslink density contains on the order of 10 millimoles of disulfide bonds, representing a thermal absorption capacity of roughly **2.5 kJ** before significant backbone decomposition occurs.

This is a chemical heat sink: the casing absorbs heat by bond-breaking, slowing the temperature rise at the propellant interface. The critical zone is the propellant–casing interface, which is exactly where cook-off risk is highest.

### Layer 3: Ablative Hydrophobic Outer Layer

The hydrophobic surface proteins — leucine, phenylalanine — are thermally more stable under oxidative conditions than polar residues. The outer surface of the casing therefore presents a slightly higher effective ignition barrier to radiant heat from the chamber walls than the propellant core, creating a useful temperature gradient.

### Layer 4: Weapon-Level Controls

No ammunition-based thermal management strategy is sufficient for indefinitely sustained high-rate fire. The BPC weapon system includes:
- **Ceramic or zirconia-coated chamber walls** to reduce heat transfer from the barrel to the chambered round
- **Lightweight barrel liner** with low thermal mass
- **Barrel temperature sensor and cyclic rate governor** that enforces a firing pause when the barrel approaches a preset temperature threshold

The BPC system's higher spontaneous ignition temperature relative to standard NC provides extra engineering margin that makes the weapon-level thermal controls less demanding than they would be for a conventional NC-based caseless design.

## 8. Component 4: The Ignition System

### Overview: Dual-Mode

The BPC ignition system has **two independent pathways**:

| Mode | Mechanism | Required hardware |
|---|---|---|
| Primary — Mechanical | Firing pin strikes percussion primer surface on the aft face of the round | Standard striker-fired bolt |
| Secondary — Electrothermal | Current pulse through embedded CNT network generates heat to initiate propellant | Battery + capacitor + electrode contacts on bolt face |

The system works in mechanical-only mode in any standard weapon. The electronic pathway is an optional upgrade. If the electronics fail, the mechanical system still fires normally. This redundancy directly addresses the failure mode of both the Remington EtronX and the Voere VEC-91 — both of which lost all firing capability when their battery systems failed.

---

### Primary Ignition: Mechanical Percussion

#### How a Conventional Primer Works

In a normal cartridge, the primer is a small brass cup filled with a percussion-sensitive compound (typically lead styphnate) pressed between a cup and an anvil. When the firing pin hits the cup, the compound is crushed between cup and anvil and explodes. The resulting hot gas and flame pass through a flash hole into the main propellant charge and ignite it.

#### How the BPC Primer Works

The BPC round has no separate primer cup — there is no brass base to mount one in. Instead, the **aft face of the propellant block** is impregnated during moulding with a percussion-sensitive compound at the surface.

The process:
1. During moulding of the propellant block, the rear ~2mm of the mould cavity contains a suspension of percussion-sensitive compound in the protein matrix slurry
2. When cured, the percussion-sensitive material is embedded within the protein matrix at the aft face — like raisins in a dough, but distributed as a thin surface layer
3. A thin (50–100 μm) protein seal layer covers the primer surface to protect it during handling and feeding
4. When the firing pin strikes, it punches through this thin protective layer and impacts the percussion surface, initiating the primer compound
5. The flame jet propagates through the propellant block, initiating combustion

#### The Firing Pin Geometry

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Striker probe diameter | 1.2–1.5 mm |
| Stroke travel | 2–3 mm |
| Required impact kinetic energy | 8–12 J (standard striker-fired rifle spec) |
| Protective seal layer thickness | 50–100 μm |

Because the BPC casing's aft face is protein rather than brass, there is no **primer flow** — the failure mode in conventional brass-cased ammunition where the primer material extrudes rearward into the firing pin hole under overpressure. The protein matrix ablates cleanly during firing.

#### Primer Compound Options

| Option | Notes |
|---|---|
| Lead styphnate | Proven, sensitive, reliable — but contains lead |
| Diazonium salt compound | Lead-free alternative — under development in industry |
| Tetrazene-based | Another lead-free option — less sensitive, may need larger striker energy |

For a lead-free BPC system, a diazonium or tetrazene priming compound is the target. This is consistent with the overall design goal of clean combustion products.

---

### Secondary Ignition: Carbon Nanotube Electrothermal

#### What Carbon Nanotubes Are

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are cylinders of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice, with diameters in the nanometre range. Single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) are among the best electrical and thermal conductors known:

| Property | SWCNT value |
|---|---|
| Electrical conductivity | >10⁶ S/m (comparable to copper) |
| Thermal conductivity | ~3,500 W/m·K (higher than diamond) |
| Diameter | 0.4–2.0 nm |

A network of SWCNTs embedded in the protein matrix acts as a miniature resistive heating element. Pass current through it and it heats up very rapidly, very locally.

#### How the CNT Ignition Works

A network of SWCNTs is embedded within the aft 1–2 mm of the propellant block, co-located with the percussion primer surface. The network is configured to present a resistance of approximately **5–10 Ω** to the firing circuit.

The weapon's bolt face has two small electrode contacts (insulated from each other and from the casing body) that press against corresponding contact points on the aft face of the BPC round when the bolt is in battery.

When the trigger is pulled electronically:
1. A battery and capacitor in the weapon discharge through the electrode contacts
2. Current flows through the CNT network
3. Joule heating raises the temperature of the network rapidly: **Q = I²Rt**
   - At I = 10 A, R = 7 Ω, t = 2 ms: Q = (10)² × 7 × 0.002 = **1.4 J**
4. This is sufficient to raise the primer surface temperature above initiation threshold in under 1 ms
5. The primer initiates and the propellant ignites normally

The CNT network is completely consumed during firing — no residue.

#### Electronic Ignition Advantages

| Advantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| Lock time | ~27 microseconds vs ~3–4 ms mechanical — 100× faster |
| Smart weapon integration | Firing can be electronically inhibited / permitted |
| Biometric safety | Bolt-integrated fingerprint reader can gate the firing circuit |
| Fire control precision | Sub-millisecond firing timing for burst synchronisation |
| Silent operation (future) | Electronic initiation can be used without audible mechanical striker impact |

#### Battery and Power Requirements

A basic implementation uses a **9V alkaline battery** feeding a **small capacitor** — essentially the same architecture as the Remington EtronX. A single 9V battery provides approximately 3–5 Wh of energy. At ~1.4 J per shot, this gives roughly **7,000–12,000 shots per battery**. In a combat scenario, battery replacement is a manageable logistics requirement and can be integrated with existing weapon battery systems (optics, suppressors).

## 9. Chamber Sealing — The Hardest Part

### Why This Is Hard

When a conventional round fires, the brass case expands under pressure and presses against the chamber walls, sealing combustion gases from venting back into the bolt face and mechanism. This is called **obturation**. Without a brass case, nothing automatically expands to provide this seal.

The G11 solved this with an extraordinarily complex rotating cylindrical chamber mechanism — the round was enclosed on all sides within the chamber before firing. It worked, but required a weapon described as "the inside of a compact clock."

### The BPC Solution

The BPC approach leverages the **elastic compliance of the protein matrix** to achieve obturation at the bolt face, without requiring a rotating chamber.

The bolt face has a recessed cavity that matches the profile of the BPC round's aft face. When the bolt closes and locks:
1. The annular rim of the bolt face seats against the outer edge of the BPC round's aft face
2. The bolt closure force (typically 200–500 N in a standard rifle bolt) compresses the protein matrix surface slightly — it deforms elastically, like a soft polymer O-ring
3. This elastic deformation creates a gas-tight seal around the perimeter of the contact surface
4. At the centre, the bolt face is recessed; only the rim contacts the round

This is conceptually similar to how polymer-cased telescoped ammunition (Textron CT) achieves gas sealing — the polymer case base provides a compliant seal against the bolt face rather than expanding like brass.

### Forward Seal

The forward end of the propellant block does not need special sealing. The bullet's driving band (the copper ring around the bullet's base) seats in the rifling and provides the forward gas seal in the conventional manner. The propellant block behind it is contained within the chamber bore.

### Dimensional Requirements

For this sealing approach to work reliably, the BPC round's aft face must be dimensionally consistent to:

| Dimension | Tolerance |
|---|---|
| Aft face outer diameter | ±0.02 mm |
| Aft face flatness | ±0.01 mm |
| Surface roughness | <Ra 0.8 μm (smooth enough for gas-tight seal) |

Injection moulding from a precision steel tool can achieve all of these. This is the same level of dimensional precision as quality polymer optical components — achievable but not trivial.

## 11. The Weapon System

### What Changes in the Weapon

A BPC weapon is adapted from a conventional gas-operated rotating bolt design. The key mechanical differences:

| Feature | Conventional weapon | BPC weapon |
|---|---|---|
| Extractor | Required | Not required (nothing to extract) |
| Ejection port | Required | Eliminated — sealed receiver |
| Bolt face | Flat, with extractor groove | Recessed annular rim, two electrode contacts |
| Firing pin | Standard centre-fire pin | Striker probe 1.2–1.5mm dia, 8–12 J |
| Chamber coating | Optional | Ceramic/zirconia coating recommended |
| Barrel temperature sensor | Optional | Recommended |
| Battery compartment | N/A | Small — 9V, stock or pistol grip mount |

### What Stays the Same

The gas operating system, barrel, trigger group (with minor modification for electronic trigger option), magazine interface, and overall ergonomic form factor are all compatible with existing military rifle design conventions. The BPC weapon is not as radically different from a standard rifle as the G11 was.

### Magazine

BPC rounds stack in a conventional box magazine with appropriate geometry for the shorter overall length. Because the rounds have no extractor groove and no brass case, the magazine's feed lips and follower geometry differ from a standard STANAG magazine. The sealed receiver means the magazine well must seal when a magazine is inserted — a simple rubber gasket accomplishes this.

### Maintenance

Because there is no ejection port, the receiver interior is largely protected from environmental contamination. The main maintenance requirements are:
- **Barrel cleaning**: reduced fouling vs. standard NC, but still required
- **Electrode contact cleaning**: CNT residue on bolt face contacts may accumulate over ~500 rounds — wipe clean with a solvent cloth
- **Barrel temperature sensor calibration**: annual or per manufacturer specification
- **Battery replacement**: approximately every 7,000–12,000 rounds

## 12. How It's Made — Manufacturing Process

### Step 1: Protein Synthesis (Bioreactor)

Recombinant spidroin proteins are produced in **E. coli bacteria** in large fermentation tanks (bioreactors). The bacteria are genetically engineered to carry the spidroin gene sequence, which includes the elevated cysteine content and hydrophobic surface domain designed for the BPC casing.

The bacteria are fed a nutrient broth and grown to high density. When protein expression is induced, they produce and accumulate the spider silk protein. After fermentation:
- Bacteria are lysed (broken open)
- Protein is extracted from the cell debris
- Purified to the required structural grade (no biomedical purity needed — simpler than pharmaceutical production)
- Concentrated into a gel or slurry

Current best-in-class E. coli yields: **14–20 g of purified protein per litre of fermentation broth** (after downstream processing).

Alternatively, **transgenic silkworms** (Kraig Biocraft platform) produce the protein as part of their silk filament, which is then processed. This platform currently achieves production costs below USD 300/kg — approximately 10× cheaper than fermentation.

### Step 2: Propellant Synthesis

A separate recombinant polypeptide is produced (or the same spidroin backbone can be used with different modification chemistry). After purification:

1. Polypeptide is dissolved in reaction solvent
2. **Nitration step**: nitric acid mixture reacts with arginine and serine/threonine side chains to introduce nitrate ester and guanidinium nitrate groups
3. **Azidation step**: lysine side chains are treated with azide-transfer reagent to introduce –N₃ groups
4. Product is carefully washed, neutralised, and dried
5. Oxygen balance and burn rate are measured; composition is adjusted by blending different modification batches

This step requires **explosion-proof infrastructure** and regulatory compliance (ATF licence in the U.S., equivalent in other jurisdictions). It is chemically analogous to industrial nitrocellulose production.

### Step 3: Casing Moulding

The purified casing protein slurry is:
1. Loaded into an **injection moulding machine**
2. Injected under pressure into a precision steel mould cavity shaped to the BPC round geometry
3. UV light or a chemical catalyst triggers the disulfide crosslinking reaction, curing the protein into a rigid solid
4. The mould is opened and the casing is ejected — dimensionally precise and ready for assembly

### Step 4: Propellant Block Casting

The nitrated propellant compound is:
1. Dissolved or plasticised into a castable consistency
2. Poured or injected into the interior cavity of the casing
3. A second moulding step forms the aft face of the round, incorporating the percussion-sensitive primer compound and the CNT ignition network in the final 2mm

This is analogous to how composite solid rocket propellant is cast into a motor case.

### Step 5: Projectile Seating

A standard copper-jacketed or solid copper bullet is pressed into the mouth of the completed propellant-in-casing assembly. This is mechanically similar to conventional bullet seating in a case neck, adapted for the protein matrix interface.

### Step 6: Quality Control

Each round is:
- Dimensionally inspected (laser or CMM measurement)
- Weight-checked
- Sampled for burn rate and pressure testing (destructive, statistical sampling)
- Subjected to environmental conditioning per applicable military specifications before batch release

## 13. Scale-Up: From Lab to Factory

### The Challenge in Numbers

Consider supplying a single military force with 100 million rounds per year (a moderate procurement quantity for a mid-size NATO army):

- Casing protein needed: **300,000–500,000 kg/year**
- Current global synthetic spider silk production: estimated **hundreds of kg to low tonnes/year**
- Required scale-up: approximately **1,000×**

This sounds daunting. But it is not unprecedented in industrial biotechnology.

### Precedents for Radical Scale-Up

| Technology | First demonstration | Global production scale achieved | Time to scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recombinant insulin | 1978 (Genentech) | Multi-tonne/year global | ~15–20 years |
| Recombinant citric acid | 1920s pilot | >2 million tonnes/year | ~40 years |
| Penicillin | 1940 (laboratory) | >50,000 tonnes/year | ~20 years |

The BPC programme requires spider silk protein to follow a similar trajectory. The key difference is that the BPC casing has **no biomedical purity requirement** — no sterility, no endotoxin limits, no viral clearance. This dramatically reduces downstream processing complexity and cost compared to pharmaceutical protein production, potentially compressing the scale-up timeline.

### Current Production Landscape (2024–2025)

| Company | Platform | Current scale | Cost ($/kg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kraig Biocraft Laboratories | Transgenic silkworm | Metric-tonne scale; 10 MT targeted 2025 | <$300 |
| Spiber Inc. (Japan) | Yeast fermentation | 500-tonne/year Thai plant (commercial 2025) | Est. $500–1,500 |
| AMSilk GmbH (Germany) | E. coli fermentation | Lower volume, medical focus | Est. $2,000–5,000 |
| Bolt Threads (U.S.) | Yeast fermentation | Commercial/apparel focus | Not disclosed |

Kraig Biocraft's transgenic silkworm platform is the most cost-competitive by approximately one order of magnitude relative to fermentation competitors. The company has U.S. Army funding for ballistic materials, directly adjacent to the BPC use case, and is actively expanding mulberry cultivation and rearing centre infrastructure in Southeast Asia.

### Scale-Up Strategy

A BPC programme would pursue a parallel-track scale-up:

**Track A — Silkworm farming expansion** (Kraig Biocraft model)
- Scale mulberry cultivation area and silkworm rearing centres in Southeast Asia
- Achieve 1,000+ tonne/year capacity within 10–15 years
- Cost projection: <$100/kg at 1,000-tonne scale (experience curve extrapolation)

**Track B — Fermentation optimisation** (E. coli or yeast)
- Target >50 g/L fermentation titre (current best is 14–20 g/L)
- Reduce downstream processing steps using industrial-grade (not pharmaceutical-grade) protocols
- Build 150 m³ bioreactor facilities modelled on Spiber's Thai plant architecture

At $100/kg casing protein cost, the protein input per 5.56mm-class BPC round is approximately $0.04 — below the material cost of the equivalent brass case.

## 17. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Does this exist yet?**  
No. The BPC system is a theoretical framework supported by published science in each of its component domains (spider silk production, energetic polymers, electronic ignition, caseless weapon design), but it has not been built or tested in any form. It is currently at TRL 1.

**Q: Is this just a version of the G11?**  
No. The G11 used a solid block of synthetic nitramine propellant as both the case and the propellant — one material doing everything. The BPC system uses a separate structural protein casing and a separate energetic propellant, each independently optimised. It also uses a standard bolt-face sealing approach rather than the G11's rotating chamber. The result should be a simpler weapon and a more robust round.

**Q: What stops the round from going off when you drop it?**  
The percussion primer on the BPC round's aft face requires a concentrated impact energy of 8–12 J from a pointed striker probe. A drop impact distributes force across the full aft face surface — much lower pressure per unit area than a striker. Additionally, the protective protein seal layer adds a threshold to mechanical initiation. The CNT network has an electrostatic discharge safety threshold set well above any ESD likely from handling. Normal drops, impacts, and even moderate crushing forces should not initiate the round.

**Q: What happens if the battery dies in the weapon?**  
The mechanical ignition system works independently of the electronic system. A dead battery means no electronic firing capability, but the weapon fires normally via mechanical striker. This is the critical operational advantage over the Remington EtronX and Voere VEC-91.

**Q: Can a BPC weapon fire conventional cased ammunition?**  
No, and this is a real interoperability challenge. A BPC weapon is designed around a fundamentally different round geometry and sealing mechanism. This means that any military transitioning to BPC would need to manage a mixed inventory during the transition period. This is not unprecedented — the transition from .30-06 to 7.62mm NATO and then to 5.56mm also required weapon and ammunition fleet management — but it is a significant logistics and procurement consideration.

**Q: Is the spider silk protein food-grade or toxic?**  
Spider silk proteins are biocompatible — they are used in biomedical implants and sutures. The casing protein is not toxic. The nitrated propellant compound, however, is an energetic material and must be handled with appropriate explosive safety procedures, the same as any propellant or explosive material.

**Q: Why hasn't anyone done this before?**  
Two reasons: (1) recombinant spider silk production at any meaningful scale did not exist until the last 5–10 years; and (2) the combination of using a protein matrix as both a structural casing and a thermal management element is a novel design concept that has not previously been proposed in the published caseless ammunition literature. The enabling technology (scalable recombinant silk production) is only now becoming commercially available.

**Q: How does this compare to Textron's cased telescoped ammunition?**  
Textron's CT ammunition still has a case — a polymer/composite case — so it is not caseless. CT significantly reduces case weight (polymer vs. brass) but retains an ejection cycle. The BPC round is fully caseless with no ejection. Ballistically and operationally they address similar goals (weight reduction, modernised small arms), but through different architectures. CT is further along the TRL scale and is in active procurement evaluation; BPC is an earlier-stage concept with greater potential performance benefits and greater development risk.

## 18. Glossary

**Ablative:** A material that protects against heat by slowly burning, melting, or evaporating — carrying heat away with it rather than conducting it inward.

**Azide group (–N₃):** A functional group containing three nitrogen atoms. When added to a polymer backbone, it increases the polymer's nitrogen content and energy density. Used in GAP and related energetic binders.

**Bolt face:** The front surface of the bolt — the part that presses against the base of the cartridge when the action is closed.

**Carbon nanotube (CNT):** A cylinder of carbon atoms with nanometre-scale diameter. Has extraordinary electrical and thermal conductivity. Used in the BPC system as a resistive heating element for electrothermal ignition.

**Caseless ammunition:** Ammunition with no metallic cartridge case. The propellant (and ideally all components except the projectile) is consumed during firing.

**Cook-off:** The premature ignition of a chambered round caused by heat from the weapon's chamber, without a trigger pull.

**Cysteine:** An amino acid whose side chain contains a thiol group (–SH). When two cysteine residues are close together, their thiol groups can form a disulfide bond (–S–S–), chemically linking the two protein chains.

**Deflagration:** Rapid combustion that propagates at subsonic speed — what happens when a propellant burns. Distinguished from detonation (supersonic shock wave). Propellants deflagrate; explosives detonate.

**Disulfide bond (R–S–S–R):** A covalent chemical bond between two sulphur atoms, formed from two cysteine residues. Bond dissociation energy ~251 kJ/mol. Forms the crosslinks in the BPC protein matrix.

**Driving band:** A ring of soft metal (usually copper) around the base of a projectile that engages the barrel's rifling, imparting spin and sealing propellant gases.

**ECR (E. coli fed-batch):** A fermentation technique where bacteria grow in a nutrient medium with continuous feeding to maintain high cell density and productivity.

**Endothermic:** A chemical reaction or process that absorbs heat from its surroundings (the opposite of exothermic). Disulfide bond cleavage is endothermic.

**Energetic material:** Any material that contains significant stored chemical energy that can be rapidly released through combustion, deflagration, or detonation. Includes propellants, explosives, and pyrotechnics.

**GAP (glycidyl azide polymer):** A well-characterised energetic binder used in military propellants. Density ~1.30 g/cm³, heat of formation +117.2 kcal/mol, burning rate 1 cm/s at 40 atm. The conceptual reference point for the BPC propellant chemistry.

**HITP (High Ignition Temperature Propellant):** The propellant formulation developed by Dynamit Nobel for the HK G11 that raised the spontaneous ignition temperature of the caseless round by approximately 100°C above standard NC, solving the G11's early cook-off problems.

**Injection moulding:** A manufacturing process where molten or fluid material is injected under pressure into a closed mould, allowed to solidify, and then ejected. Used for the BPC casing.

**Joule heating:** The production of heat by passing electrical current through a resistive conductor. Quantified by Q = I²Rt (heat energy = current squared × resistance × time).

**Lock time:** The time between pulling the trigger and the bullet exiting the barrel. Mechanical striker systems: 3–4 ms. Electronic ignition systems: ~27 microseconds.

**Nitrate ester (–ONO₂):** A functional group formed by nitrating a hydroxyl group. The key energetic group in nitrocellulose and nitroglycerin. Provides internal oxygen supply for combustion.

**Nitrocellulose (NC):** The primary component of smokeless gunpowder. Made by nitrating cellulose with nitric acid. Nitrogen content 12.6–13.8% for propellant grade.

**Obturation:** The sealing of the breech of a firearm against rearward escape of propellant gases when the weapon fires. In cased ammunition, the expanding brass case provides obturation.

**Oxygen balance (Ω%):** A measure of whether an energetic material contains enough internal oxygen to completely combust. Target for BPC propellant: −5% to +5%.

**Percussion primer:** A small explosive compound that initiates when struck. Standard small arms priming compounds include lead styphnate (lead-containing) and various lead-free alternatives.

**Recombinant protein:** A protein produced by a host organism (bacteria, yeast, silkworm) that has been genetically engineered to carry a foreign gene. Recombinant spider silk proteins are produced this way since spiders cannot be farmed at scale.

**Spidroin:** The protein that makes up spider silk. The term covers the various types produced by different spider glands (major ampullate spidroin, minor ampullate spidroin, etc.).

**TRL (Technology Readiness Level):** A nine-level scale used by DARPA and the U.S. military to measure the maturity of a technology, from TRL 1 (basic principles observed) to TRL 9 (system proven in operational environment).

**Transgenic silkworm:** A silkworm (Bombyx mori) that has been genetically modified to produce spider silk proteins instead of (or in addition to) its own silk proteins. Used by Kraig Biocraft Laboratories for low-cost spider silk production.

**Volumetric energy density:** Energy content per unit volume. Measured in MJ/L. Higher values mean more energy in a smaller space — useful for ammunition design where round dimensions are constrained.
